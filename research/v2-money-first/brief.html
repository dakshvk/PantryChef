03 — The one you kept

Water holds up. The door doesn't.

You liked the water idea. The sector survived scrutiny — but the specific entry point was already demoted last round, and the deeper scan found a better one two steps to the left.

The original idea was building school-and-daycare rosters for water utilities under the federal lead rule. It fell over because 120Water already ships that product, Rural Water Associations endorse software rather than buy it, and EPA published the free how-to guide in June 2026 telling utilities to build the roster from billing records instead.

So the new agent mapped the whole industry instead — drinking water, wastewater, stormwater, billing, water rights, wells, leak detection, grease traps, operator licensing. Its conclusion:

The money in water for one person with no capital is not in selling software to water utilities — that path is an RFP, a reference customer, and a nine-to-eighteen-month cycle. It is in selling to the thousands of small contractors the utilities regulate, who pay by credit card, whose names and phone numbers are published free on utility websites, and whose work is legally compelled.Water money map agent
1

Backflow testing software — sold to the testers, not the utilities

Buyer: the owner of a 1–5 person backflow, irrigation or plumbing shop

Card on file, same day
What it is

A phone app for a plumber who tests backflow valves. It captures the test, fills in whichever form that particular city demands, and — the part he actually pays for — tells him which of his customers are due for their annual re-test, and sends the reminder.

Why this exists at all

A backflow preventer stops dirty water flowing backwards into the public drinking supply. Every commercial building has them, and the law requires a certified tester to check each one every year. Miss it and the penalties are real: Longmont, Colorado charges up to $500 per assembly plus a scheduled water shutoff. Denver Water charges $250 and moves you to suspension. San Francisco can terminate service outright.

Three parties touch one record — the utility, the property owner, and the tester — and none of them share a system. That is why it still runs on Excel.

Why you can reach the buyer

Every water utility in America publishes its list of certified testers. Marin Water, CFPUA, James City County, Liberty Lake, SF.gov, Missouri DNR — thousands of such pages. Name, company, phone, often email. Pre-qualified as licensed and actively working.

That is a free, public, phone-verified lead list of 19,000+ certified professionals — and scraping it into a national database is itself a defensible asset.

How you'd build it
  1. Scrape 200–400 utility tester-list pages into one national database. Two weeks.
  2. Build the capture: gauge readings, pass/fail, assembly serial, photo — on a phone, in a mechanical room, offline.
  3. Auto-generate the specific PDF that specific utility requires. This is the unglamorous differentiator and nobody does it well.
  4. Build the due-date engine. The re-test reminder is the actual product — it converts a one-off job into an annuity for the tester. That is why he pays you.

Idea 1 funds idea 2 — and hands it an unfair advantage. When a utility asks "will our testers actually use this," you answer with their names. 05 — PantryChef

The consumer app is closed by arithmetic

You asked how to make it better and how to make money from it. The honest answer to the second question changes the answer to the first.

Does anyone make money selling recipe apps?

Cookpad is the only public pure-play, so its numbers are audited. It is the largest recipe platform in the world outside Allrecipes, with tens of millions of users and about two million paid subscribers at its peak.

MyFitnessPal's 2025 revenue — roughly 9× Cookpad's entire global business, on near-identical technology

The reason Cookpad fell is instructive and it applies directly: from 2016, people stopped searching and reading recipes and started watching them. The interface to recipes got disrupted. In 2026 it is being disrupted again — "what can I make with chicken, rice and half a lemon, I'm dairy-free" is now a free question to any chatbot. PantryChef would be launching after that shift, not before it.

And everyone else in the category was acquired as a funnel for something that actually monetises. Whirlpool paid $100M for Yummly in 2017, laid off the whole team in April 2024, and switched it off that December with no export path. Mealime went to a grocer. Whisk went to Samsung. Crouton — winner of an Apple Design Award, the best-designed recipe app in the world — was bought by a thermometer company. The best independent outcome anyone found is AnyList at roughly $2M ARR with two people.

The arithmetic that ends it

Food & Drink apps have the worst retention of any category in mobile: 3.9% at day 30. Freemium converts at 2.1%. Paying subscribers churn at 13–14% every month. US cost per install runs about $4.06.

CAC per paying subscriber = $4.06 ÷ 0.021 ≈ $193
LTV = ($4.99 − store cut) ÷ 0.135 churn ≈ $31

Every paid install loses about $162 — before you pay Spoonacular and Gemini on every query, including the 97.9% who never pay.

That is not a marketing problem. It's a 6× inversion. And the only channel left — organic — is the one a solo founder can neither control nor forecast.

One more structural problem specific to you: Spoonacular's terms let you cache their data for one hour. You can never accumulate a corpus. Every query is a rented API call — no compounding, nothing to license, nothing that becomes an asset. That alone caps the company's value near zero, and it's why every non-subscription model fails too: Walmart pays 0% on grocery affiliate, Amazon pays 1%, and you can't licence data you're forbidden from storing.

The pivot: point it at the walk-in cooler instead of the home fridge

The valuable thing you built is not the recipe database. It's the engine underneath: given an inventory, a set of hard constraints, and an objective, rank what's feasible and explain the substitutions.

That engine is worth about $5/month to a consumer. It is worth $79–$199 per month per location to a kitchen with a food cost line on its P&L.

"Here's what's in inventory tonight. Here's what you can put on the menu, at what plate cost, at what margin, without breaking the allergen matrix or the 86 list."

Why this specific pivot

  • The code transfers almost unchanged. Swap "mood" for "target food cost %" and "dietary restriction" for "allergen matrix and par levels." The scoring engine, substitution logic and explanation layer all survive.
  • The Spoonacular dependency doesn't survive — and that's the point. The recipes become the customer's own. You finally own a data asset instead of renting one on a one-hour lease.
  • Revenue per customer goes up 20–40×. meez sells at $79–199/location/mo; MarketMan at $199–239. Thirty locations at $150 is $54K ARR — more than six thousand consumer subscribers, and far easier to keep.
  • Retention inverts. Consumer food apps retain 3.9% at day 30. A restaurant that has entered two hundred costed recipes does not churn — the switching cost is the data entry.
  • A chef can buy it on a personal card after one demo. meez and Craftable both publish self-serve prices. No RFP, no procurement, no security review. This is the only B2B food segment where that's true.

The second-best pivot, with a real mandate behind it: dietary compliance for senior living. MealSuite starts at $175/mo and the sector transacts at $3–5 per resident per month — a 20-facility operator is $115K/yr. And "this resident is renal, diabetic and shellfish-allergic, what can they eat from tonight's menu" is literally your existing function with the mood parameter deleted.

So what do you actually do with it

Ship PantryChef as it is, write it up properly, and put it in the portfolio. It's a genuinely strong artifact of engineering judgment — deterministic scoring with the LLM confined to validation and explanation is the right architecture, and worth being able to defend in an interview.

Then spend one weekend talking to ten chefs about food cost and 86 lists before writing another line of code. If they don't bite, you've lost a weekend instead of two years and $193 a subscriber.

The procurement ceiling — design around it from day one

SOC 2 becomes effectively mandatory above roughly $50K in annual contract value, and procurement at companies with 200+ employees routinely blocks vendors without it. SOC 2 costs $45,000–70,000 in the first year.

So your market is businesses under about 200 employees, where the person you're talking to can put it on a card without asking anyone. Above that line lies security review, vendor onboarding and legal — with a $45K compliance bill and no company to put it on. Choosing a buyer whose procurement you can't survive is a named, documented way to die.

Two practical notes. Sonnet's introductory API pricing ends 1 September — route to Haiku by default and cache your system prompt; at 10% of input price that's the single biggest cost lever available, and it's the difference between viable and not at $29/mo. And confirm California's $800 franchise tax first-year waiver with the FTB directly — the sources genuinely contradict each other, and it's an $800 question.

How solo founders die

CB Insights analysed 483 startup post-mortems. "No market need" is the number one cause, and nine of the top twenty reasons relate to customers — not meeting their needs, not listening, or ignoring them.

For you specifically, the risk isn't any of the exotic ones. It's this: 54% of solo founders report burnout; 30% abandon before $1K MRR; and there is no external forcing function — no investor, no co-founder, no payroll. In a twelve-to-eighteen-month stretch with no feedback, quietly stopping is the path of least resistance, and it is what most people do.

In the early days we tried nearly every growth idea — Reddit posts, Indie Hackers updates, Slack communities, startup directories, newsletters, cold outreach. Most brought small wins but no repeatable traction.Senja.io founders, who did eventually reach $5,000 MRR

Budget for a long stretch where nothing compounds. And note what the NVC actually gives you against that: eight months of deadlines, a cohort, and sixty people expecting to see progress.

07 — If it were my call

What I'd do

  1. Go to the NVC information session in October. Five weeks out, one-in-six odds at real money, sixty mentors, and an eight-month structure aimed straight at the failure mode most likely to get you. Apply to the 1517 Medici Grant this week while you're at it.
  2. Pick one vertical and take paid service work in it. Backflow testers and small trucking carriers are both reachable this month with a free public list and a phone. This pays in weeks and buys the sector experience worth +125%.
  3. Buy a $300 conference badge before you write any code. Two hundred buyers in one hotel, and student status is an asset in that room rather than a liability.
  4. Do the first ten jobs by hand. Tell customers it's manual — the research is clear that they pay anyway. Automate only what three unrelated clients have already paid you to do.
  5. Ship PantryChef as a portfolio piece and stop developing it. Revisit the engine only if ten chefs tell you food cost is a real problem worth $150/month.
  6. Keep burn at $110/month and never sell to anyone whose security team you'd have to survive.

The through-line: every idea on this page is reachable because someone publishes a list — certified backflow testers, new trucking authorities, permitted facilities, licensed food producers. You have no network, and a public list is the closest thing to one that money can't buy. That, more than any single idea here, is the asset to build on.
