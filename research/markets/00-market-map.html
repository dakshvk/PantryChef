"Environmental data → activity-specific score" is the most commoditised product pattern in consumer software right now.

A weather API costs nothing, the heuristic is a weekend, and AI coding tools mean anyone with an idea ships it in days. Every activity has three to six of these. The ones that look empty will have five within a year. This is why your gap-hunting keeps returning the same answer — you're hunting in the one category where the barrier to entry is approximately zero.

The conversion number is the one to internalise

AllTrails converts at 5–7%. That is the shape of consumer outdoor software: you need enormous top-of-funnel to reach modest revenue. Sixty-five million users produce roughly $130M. A niche conditions app with 5,000 users and a 5% conversion at $30/year is $7,500 a year. Not a business — a hobby with a Stripe account.

All three winners own a dataset or a network. None of them owns a forecast.

Conditions prediction is a feature — something each winner added after they had distribution, and something dozens of solo developers ship as standalone apps that go nowhere. onX is the counter-example worth studying: they won by doing the boring, expensive, unglamorous work of assembling parcel records nobody else would touch.
The question to ask instead

Stop hunting activities. Start hunting datasets.

Wrong question: "Which outdoor activity doesn't have a conditions app yet?"

Right question: "What data could I assemble that nobody else has bothered to, that someone needs to make a decision?"

The first question has been answered exhaustively by hundreds of solo developers with the same weather APIs you'd use. The second is what onX answered, and it's why they have five million paying subscribers while five climbing-condition apps split a few thousand users between them.

The tests a dataset has to pass

  • Is it hard to assemble? If it comes from a public API, everyone has it. onX's parcel data required county-by-county acquisition — that difficulty is the moat.
  • Does it get better with use? Fishbrain's 7 million logged catches make its model better and a new entrant's worse. That's compounding.
  • Does someone make a decision with money attached? A hunter who trespasses faces real consequences. A climber who picks a humid day is mildly annoyed.
  • Would it still be valuable if the weather API disappeared? If no, you have a wrapper.

⚠️ I have not researched which specific datasets are unassembled. That's the next research pass, and it's a different search — you look for records that exist but are fragmented, not for apps that are missing.

What I'd actually do

Three moves, in order

1. Run the "does it exist" check first, always

Five markets, five kills, one search each. That check costs ten minutes and it has now saved you five times. Put it above every idea in your notes, before the market sizing and before the technical plan. It is the cheapest filter you own and you keep applying it last.

2. Do one dataset-hunting pass instead of one more idea-hunting pass

Look for records that exist but are fragmented — the shape onX found. Public but scattered. Legally accessible but nobody has assembled them. In your world that might be: dive site access and entry information, marine protected area boundaries against actual enforcement, shore access rights, water quality sampling records, small-harbour and launch data. I don't know which of these is unassembled — that's the next research pass, and it's a different search than the one that keeps failing.

The test: could I still charge for this if every weather API went away tomorrow?

3. Keep the sonar project exactly where you put it

Your instinct to move it to a separate "deep tech research" board is right. It is the one thing in this entire programme that survived every check — because it isn't competing on a weather API, it's competing on something genuinely nobody has done. It's a research project on a different clock, and it costs $0 to start. The two tracks don't compete for the same hours.

You've now run this loop enough times to see its shape: the ideas that come from public information lose to the dozens of people who had the same information.

Fishbrain has 7 million logged catches. onX has county parcel records. AllTrails has a decade of reviews. Every winner in this category is sitting on something that took years of unglamorous accumulation — and that's the only thing in consumer outdoor software that a weekend project can't replicate.
