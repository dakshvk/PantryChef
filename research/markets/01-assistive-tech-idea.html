Why this discipline

Two crises that turn out to be one crisis

Veterinary prices are rising far faster than everything else — urban veterinary services rose 5.9% from March 2024 to March 2025 against 2.4% for all consumer goods, and costs at some practices have climbed as much as 40%. Meanwhile the workforce is short and burning out.

"Telling clients you can't help them because they have no money is one of the leading causes of burnout in the veterinary profession."

That single sentence connects the two. The affordability crisis and the burnout crisis are the same crisis, experienced from opposite sides of the exam table — and it has a name in the profession: economic euthanasia.

And unlike almost everything else in this research programme, discovery here is trivial. There is a veterinary clinic within walking distance of you right now, the people in it will talk about this specific thing at length, and it is the emotional centre of their working life.

But the check surfaced something better than a kill

A 2026 paper in Frontiers in Veterinary Science — "Lack of online price transparency of small animal veterinary clinics in the United States" — found that price information on clinic websites is incomplete or inconsistently presented, and that many clinics fail to provide basic pricing for common procedures at all.

US veterinary pricing is largely self-regulated, with no federal requirement to disclose fees. Professional guidance asks for transparent communication between vet and client, but never requires a published price list.

So three comparison apps are scraping a surface that mostly doesn't exist. The interface isn't the hard part. The dataset is.

This is precisely the situation onX was in before onX: everyone knew parcel data would be valuable, and nobody had done the unglamorous work of assembling it. Vet Receipt's approach — verified invoices submitted by owners — is the correct shape, and the fact that someone has started is a signal the shape is right, not that the space is closed.
The idea

Not "what does a spay cost." The 11pm question.

Your dog is vomiting blood at 11pm. The emergency vet hands you an estimate for $4,000. You have $900. Nobody in the world can tell you what happens on each path.

Price comparison is a planned purchase tool — useful for a dental, useless in an emergency, and emergencies are where the $2,500 ceiling gets crossed and pets get euthanised for money. The decision that destroys people is not "which clinic," it's "which version of care, and what am I giving up."

What you'd actually build

A dataset of what real treatments actually cost, what owners actually chose, and what actually happened — assembled from owner-submitted invoices and follow-ups, normalised into comparable procedures.

// what an invoice line actually looks like
ANES-ISO 15MIN   IVC PLCMT 22G   RADS 2V ABD   CBC/CHEM17   HOSP LVL2 O/N

// what it has to become
general anaesthesia · IV catheter · abdominal radiographs, 2 view · bloodwork panel · overnight hospitalisation, level 2
Thousands of practices. Dozens of practice management systems. No standard.

That normalisation is the whole moat, and it is a genuine language-model job. Not a wrapper — messy, abbreviated, practice-specific clinical shorthand into a comparable schema, at scale, cheaply. It is the boring, ugly work that nobody wants to do, which is exactly why it's worth doing.

1

Owners submit invoices

They already have them. The incentive to submit is immediate: "see what other people paid for this." That is a reason to upload before the dataset is any good — which solves the cold start.

2

The model normalises them

Line items into standard procedures, with prices, geography and clinic type attached.

3

Follow up on outcome

One question, weeks later: what did you choose, and how is your pet? This is the part nobody has, and it's the part that makes the dataset irreplaceable — because it converts a price list into a record of consequences.

4

Give it back at the moment of the decision

Not a recommendation. A distribution: for this presentation, 340 owners reported estimates between $2,100 and $5,400. 61% pursued the full workup. Of those who chose the medical-management path instead, here is what they reported.

The invariant — same discipline as your allergen engine

It never recommends a course of action. It never mentions euthanasia. It never predicts an outcome for this animal. It reports what other owners paid, what they chose, and what they said happened — and it says loudly that a veterinarian is the only person who can advise on a specific case.

Cross that line and you are practising veterinary medicine without a licence, in the most emotionally charged decision a pet owner ever makes. The product is a mirror held up to the population, not an oracle pointed at one animal.

Where the money is — and it isn't the pet owner

The honest business model

The trap to avoid: pet owners who can't afford veterinary care also can't afford your subscription. That's the same failure as the spearfishing HUD — the people who need it most are the least able to pay. Do not build a consumer subscription on this.

Consumer-facing stays free. It's how the dataset gets built.

Before anything else

What I did not verify, in order

1

Look hard at Vet Receipt

They already do verified invoices. How many? Do they normalise line items or just store totals? Do they collect outcomes? If they collect outcomes, this idea is theirs and you should stop. That is one afternoon of work and it comes before everything else.

2

Does the outcome question actually get answered?

Price data is easy to collect; outcomes are not. Owners whose pet died may never reply, which biases the whole dataset toward survivors. This is the hardest unsolved thing in the plan and I have no evidence either way.

3

Cold start maths

How many invoices before a given presentation in a given metro has enough data to say anything? I don't know. It might be thousands. Estimate it before building.

4

Talk to five vets and five owners

Vets first — ask what they did the last time a client couldn't pay. Then owners who declined care. If "I had no idea what my options were" isn't what comes back, the thesis is wrong.

And the thing that could sink it

Veterinarians may hate this. A public record of what practices charge, cross-referenced against outcomes, is not something the profession has asked for — and the profession is exhausted, underpaid relative to its debt load, and already dealing with client aggression over pricing. If it reads as an attack on vets, it fails, deservedly. The framing that survives is the one the data actually supports: the same tool that helps an owner understand their options is the tool that stops a vet having to have the worst conversation in their job unarmed.

The reusable part

Why this one surfaced and twenty outdoor markets didn't

Run the filter: does this market have something in it that makes normal people give up?

  • A documented failure rate — 52% deferring care, pets dying over money
  • Data that is genuinely hard to assemble — no disclosure requirement, no standard format, thousands of practice management systems
  • An emotionally brutal subject that most founders would rather not sit with
  • Real liability if you get the framing wrong
  • But: zero hardware, trivial discovery, and a language model doing exactly what language models are good at

Compare that with a climbing conditions app: free weather API, a weekend of work, five competitors, no barrier at all. The friction is the whole opportunity. Every market this filter rejects is one where the ideas are cheap and so is the competition.
