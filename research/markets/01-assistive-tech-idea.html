What you've been walking past

You have an asset. You've been hunting for ideas instead of a buyer for it.

PantryChef's safety architecture

Three layers — Hard Executioner, Intolerance Auditor, Gemini Safety Jury — built on an invariant you articulated yourself: the model may narrow the safe set, never widen it. An LLM can never move an ingredient from unsafe to safe.

Across all this research, no competitor had that. Eatvora has the inventory loop but no deterministic allergen handling. MarketMan ships AI recipe generation from a photographed ingredient list and nothing about safety. The six AI fish-ID apps, the five gardening apps, Fishbrain — none of them are load-bearing for anyone's life, so none of them needed it. You solved a problem the consumer market didn't have.

That's why the consumer framing kept failing. Recipe app users won't pay for guaranteed allergen safety because the downside of getting it wrong is mild. You built a seatbelt and tried to sell it to people going for a walk.

Lead with the money this time

Someone does have that downside, and they're regulated

Institutional food service — schools, care homes, hospitals, contract caterers, food manufacturers. Here the failure mode is a dead child and a lawsuit, and the environment is tightening right now.

These are the conditions that were missing from restaurant food waste — where the fine was $50–200 with a 60-day cure window and nothing compelled the spend.

A mandated document, a two-year retention duty, a tightening enforcement posture, and a $150k–$5M downside. That's a budget line that already exists.

Compare it with everything that died: the restaurant waste mandate carried a $100 fine with a cure period, the vet idea had no payer at all, and the outdoor apps had no downside to getting it wrong. This is the first market in the whole programme where somebody is legally obliged to do the thing you already built.

And it reuses what exists, rather than starting over

  • The ingredient ontology with canonical IDs and alias tables — the thing that makes allergen decisions a database lookup rather than a guess
  • The deterministic engine and its never-widen invariant, which is exactly the audit story a regulator wants
  • The schema.org recipe parsing work, repointed at supplier spec sheets and product labels instead of food blogs
  • Every decision is already logged and explainable — which is the FSMA documentation requirement, produced as a by-product

You would be changing the buyer, not the codebase.

Before you get excited

What I did not check — and it's the same check that killed the other six

  • Allergen management software already exists. I saw Miratag and Alleratech in the results and did not investigate either. Run the full check before anything else — and specifically on the institutional and school segment, not restaurants.
  • Is the mandated document actually a pain? Schools may already have a compliant binder that nobody wants to replace. A legal duty is not the same as a felt problem.
  • Who signs? School district food service directors, not principals. Procurement is slow and budget cycles are annual.
  • Does the liability argument sell, or scare? "Your current process could kill a child" is true and is also a terrible sales pitch.
  • The claimed metrics in your README — 98.7% dietary safety accuracy — would need real validation if anyone's liability rests on them. That's a different standard of evidence than a class project.

⚠️ I am flagging this as a lead, not a verdict. Six ideas died on exactly this step and this one has not been through it.
