Three reasons nobody built it

And one of them is the interesting one

⚠️ These are my reasoning, not research findings. No source told me why the gap exists. Treat them as hypotheses to test, not conclusions.

1. Assigned topics fight motivation

Every Feynman app is bring-your-own-topic, and that's probably not an oversight — people study what they already need to know. Being told what to learn on a schedule is what school does, and a lot of people are paying to escape it. The discovery apps get away with it because reading a 3-minute article costs nothing. Asking someone to prepare and present on a topic they didn't choose is a very different demand.

2. The retention maths are brutal

Frictionless consumption apps already struggle to hold daily users. Adding "now speak for five minutes about it" is one of the largest asks in consumer software — it needs privacy, a quiet room, and a willingness to hear you were wrong. Daily is almost certainly the wrong cadence.

⭐ 3. Grading content mastery on an arbitrary topic is genuinely hard

This is the real one. Notice how the Feynman apps dodge it: they grade your explanation against source material you supplied. The ground truth walks in the door with you.

If the app assigns the topic, the app has to own the ground truth — and it has to be right about a different obscure subject every time, accurate enough to tell a student they're wrong. Yoodli sidesteps this entirely by grading only how you spoke. Nobody has taken on "was what you said about this niche topic actually correct," because that's the part that's hard.

Your addition changes this

Supplying the sources solves objection three

"It gives you credible sources to read or learn from" isn't a feature — it's the structural answer to the only hard objection.

If the app hands you the reading, the app owns the ground truth by construction — the same trick the Feynman apps use, just moved one step earlier. You're no longer graded against the open internet on an obscure subject. You're graded against the five things it told you to read, which is a closed, checkable set.

That also makes the feedback honest in a way a general model can't be. "You said X. Source 2 says Y — here's the passage" is verifiable, and it's a completely different product from an LLM freestyling an opinion about your answer.

And it relocates the moat to somewhere useful: the source sets become the asset.

A vetted, curated collection of "here are the five best things to read about this specific niche topic," accumulated across hundreds of topics, is the closest thing in this whole programme to the onX shape — unglamorous, slow to build, and hard for a competitor to copy in a weekend. The app is the wrapper. The library is the company.

But it moves the difficulty rather than removing it

  • "Credible" is doing enormous work. An LLM choosing sources will confidently pick bad ones, and on a niche topic you won't know. Whoever or whatever vets those sources is the actual product, and it's the part that can't be automated away without becoming the thing you were trying to avoid.
  • It edges toward NerdSip, which already AI-generates courses on any topic. The distance between "generated course" and "curated sources" is exactly the credibility work — so that's the thing to be genuinely better at, and the only thing.
  • Rights and linking. Pointing at good sources is fine; reproducing them isn't. That's the same index-the-facts, link-for-the-prose line the recipe research landed on.

Why this one is different for you

You can test it on yourself this weekend

Every other idea in this programme needed someone else — restaurant owners, dive shops, school districts, veterinarians, contractors. This one needs one user, and you already have him.

  • Build the crude version in an afternoon. A script that picks a niche topic, gathers five credible sources, sets a deadline, takes your recorded explanation, and grades it against those five sources with the passage quoted back. No app, no UI, no account.
  • Use it for two weeks. Not "would I use this" — did you.
  • The signal is whether you keep doing it after the novelty dies, roughly day four. If you don't, you have your answer for the price of one afternoon, and it's the same answer everyone else got.
  • If you do keep going, you've found the first thing all session that survived both the existence check and contact with a real user.

And the two things to measure are visible from day one: how often the grader is wrong about the topic it chose, and how often the sources it picked were actually any good. Those two error rates are the product. Low, and you have something nobody has assembled. High, and you've learned exactly why nobody assembled it.

Eight ideas in, this is the only one where the fastest way to the truth is building it rather than researching it.

That's not because it's the best idea — it's because the user is you, the build is an afternoon, and the failure mode is measurable. Everything else in this programme required permission from someone else before you could learn anything.
