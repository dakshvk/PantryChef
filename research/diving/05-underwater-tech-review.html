Idea #1 · The premise is backwards

Garmin is not the expensive option. Garmin is the cheap one.

Your notes frame the T2 at $200–350 as lock-in pricing. The right comparison isn't a Garmin computer — it's what an underwater acoustic transceiver costs when a company doesn't have consumer manufacturing volume.

Enterprise acoustic modemL3Harris GPM300, Sonardyne Scout — certified cybersecurity modules, extended temperature ratings, NATO STANAG-compliant encryption
$12,000–22,000+
Professional acoustic modemEvoLogics S2C TINY / Compact, Teledyne Benthos D297 — 1.5–4 kbps at 2–5 km, robust error correction
$4,500–9,500
Garmin Descent T2 transceiverAcoustic comms + tank telemetry, 30 m, 8-diver network
$200–350
Your target priceAn open universal transceiver
$150–300

Garmin is already selling an underwater acoustic transceiver for roughly 3–7% of what the cheapest industrial one costs.

They got there by having Garmin's manufacturing volume and by deliberately restricting the spec — 30 m, preset messages only, up to 45 seconds per message — to what a cheap transducer and a small battery can actually do. "Build the same thing but cheaper and open" means beating the cheapest consumer acoustic device on earth, while buying components in startup quantities. That is the wrong end of the cost curve to attack from.

And Garmin has already taken the surface-relay idea too

The Descent S1 Buoy ships: a surface buoy running SubWave that lets divers communicate through each other's dive computers and through the buoy — which is the bridge between the underwater acoustic network and everything above the surface.

Garmin describes SubWave as proprietary, and holds filings in the space including US10227116, "Transceiver devices and related communication and navigation methods." ⚠️ I could not open the patent files to assess claim scope — that's a real gap, and it's the kind of thing a patent attorney reads before you build, not after.

The good news you didn't expect

The open protocol already exists — and it's a NATO standard

JANUS, promulgated as NATO STANAG 4748 on 24 March 2017, is the first internationally adopted digital underwater acoustic communications standard.

  • Frequency-hopping BFSK, chosen for resilience against multipath, Doppler shift and low SNR — it works down to −3 dB SNR
  • Operating band 3–70 kHz, message lengths from seconds to minutes
  • Open source, GPL-licensed, with reference implementations in Matlab and C published at januswiki.org

So question 27 answers itself: you would not need to invent an open protocol, because NATO already published one and gave it away. That genuinely removes a chunk of risk from the "open layer" framing — the protocol was never the hard part. The hard part is the transducer, the power budget and the manufacturing volume.

Idea #2 · It exists, and it's older than you are

Diver-held sonar navigation has shipped since the 1990s

Shark Marine Navigator

A one-man diver-held sonar imaging and navigation system. "In use by many of the world's Navies, and has become a trusted piece of their standard kit," plus police forces, fire departments, scientific organisations and commercial dive companies.

  • Lets divers see in zero visibility and know their precise location
  • Core navigation board with inbuilt advanced dead-reckoning, on a large pressure-resistant flat screen
  • The MAKO DDS variant pairs it with a propulsion device — automated flight paths plus forward-looking sonar imagery to 300 m
  • Blueprint Subsea Artemis is a second diver-held sonar in the same category

Price: quote only — which in this industry means defence pricing. ⚠️ I could not obtain a figure.

The category isn't unbuilt. It's built, mature, and priced for navies — which is a completely different problem to solve.

"Nobody has made this" would be a research question. "This exists at $50,000 and nobody has made a $300 version" is a cost-engineering question, and the answer to it is in the price ladder above.

The bigger problem: photogrammetry already won the science use cases

Your list of applications — reef monitoring, marine biology, conservation, shipwreck mapping, underwater archaeology, scientific surveys — is already being served, and not by sonar.

  • Divers swim over a reef with a consumer action camera and learning-based Structure-from-Motion reconstructs the 3D geometry — in real time (DeepReefMap, Scientific Reports, 2025)
  • Underwater photogrammetry has grown rapidly over five years on cheap cameras, more compute, and a push for reproducible data
  • Researchers explicitly note it "does not require large or highly specialized equipment" — GoPro-class hardware plus software

A $300 camera already produces far higher-resolution 3D reef models than any diver-portable sonar could, and it is the established method in the literature. Sonar's remaining advantage is narrow and specific: zero visibility, and range beyond what light reaches. That is a real niche — search and recovery, murky harbours, low-vis inland water. It is also precisely the niche Shark Marine already sells into.

Hardware · 1–7

1

How small can an underwater sonar system realistically be?

AnsweredSmall enough. The Blue Robotics Ping360 is a compact scanning sonar rated to 300 m depth with 50 m range, designed for small ROVs. Size is not your constraint — price and scan rate are.

2

How much would the hardware cost?

Blocker$1,975 for the Ping360 alone, described by its maker as "less than half of any other scanning sonar on the market." That is a retail sonar head with no housing, battery, display, processor or transceiver. Blueprint Subsea's Oculus multibeam is quote-only, i.e. more.

3

Could a consumer device achieve useful mapping resolution?

PartlyUseful for obstacle avoidance and navigation in zero visibility — that's what the Ping360 is sold for. Not useful for the reef and archaeology detail in your applications list; photogrammetry beats it there by a wide margin.

4

What battery life is realistic?

UnverifiedNo figure obtained. But note the structural pressure: Garmin's 45-second message latency at 30 m is a power-and-size tradeoff, not a physics limit. A device that both pings continuously and transmits will draw far more than one that sends a preset every few minutes.

5

How deep could the device operate?

AnsweredNot a constraint. Ping360 is rated to 300 m; Oculus models go to 4,000 m. Recreational diving stops around 40 m.

6

How much does waterproofing add to cost/complexity?

UnverifiedNo isolated figure. Indirect evidence: it's clearly not the dominant cost, since a 300 m-rated Ping360 sells for under $2,000 — the transducer and electronics dominate, not the housing.

7

Can sonar and acoustic communication operate simultaneously?

Unverified — and it's a real riskNo direct source found. But both occupy the same acoustic channel, and JANUS was specifically designed for resilience against multipath and low SNR — which tells you the channel is already hostile before you add your own sonar pinging into it. Treat co-existence as an open engineering risk, not an assumption.

Mapping · 8–15

8

Can sonar generate a sufficiently detailed local 2D/3D map?

Yes in research, on vehiclesPublished work covers 3D mapping of submerged structures with a single-beam scanning sonar, multibeam forward-looking sonar localization, and 3D profiling sonar SLAM — the most recent published February 2026. All on ROVs and AUVs, none on a diver.

9

Could underwater sonar support something analogous to SLAM?

Yes — but not from sonar aloneState of the art is tightly-coupled fusion: DVL + IMU + camera + sonar in a graph-optimisation framework (AQUA-SLAM, VIMS, TURTLMap). The Doppler Velocity Log is the backbone, and the smallest one — Water Linked A50, 300 m rated, ±1% long-term accuracy — is quote-only priced. Sonar plus an IMU on its own is not the published approach.

10

How accurately could the system determine the diver's position?

Unverified for diversThe DVL spec is ±1% long-term accuracy — meaning ~1 m of error per 100 m travelled, which is genuinely good. Without a DVL you are back to dead reckoning with unbounded drift, which is what Shark Marine's system uses and what Garmin's watches cannot do.

11

How quickly would the map update?

This is a named blockerDirectly from the literature: real-time underwater SLAM on a small platform is limited by "the vast amount of image sonar data and acoustic equipment's inherent high latency." And a mechanical scanning sonar like the Ping360 physically rotates a head to build each frame — it is slow by construction.

12

What happens in murky water?

Your best answerThis is where sonar wins. The Ping360 is explicitly sold for "ROV navigation in poor visibility underwater environments", and Shark Marine's pitch is that it "allows divers to see in zero visibility." Optical methods fail here; acoustics don't. It is also the only defensible niche you have against photogrammetry.

13

How do sediment, bubbles, vegetation and reef structure affect sonar?

PartlyNo direct study obtained. The SLAM literature names suspended particles as a challenge, and the acoustic-channel research names multipath and Doppler — reef structure is a multipath generator by definition. ⚠️ Bubbles specifically: no source found, and a diver exhales continuously right next to the sensor. Worth checking before anything else.

14

What is the realistic range?

Answered50 m for the Ping360, up to 200 m for Oculus multibeam, 300 m for Shark Marine's forward-looking sonar. Range is not the problem.

15

Could multiple sonar-equipped divers interfere with each other?

Unverified — assume yesNo source found. But it is the same shared acoustic channel, and your own design has up to eight divers on it. Mutual interference between co-located active sonars is a classic problem, and you should treat it as one until proven otherwise.

Communication · 16–20

16

Can an open protocol compete with Garmin SubWave?

The protocol isn't the problemJANUS is technically credible and free. But protocols don't compete — products do, and Garmin's has a wrist computer, a transceiver, a buoy, retail distribution and a customer base. An open standard with no hardware behind it competes with nothing.

17

Could the system communicate with existing dive computers?

NoNot without the manufacturers' cooperation. Air-integration pairing lives in the radio protocol inside the transmitter and receiver chip. Four brands already share one standard (Oceanic, Aqualung, Sherwood and Shearwater all use Pelagic transmitters, FCC ID MH8A) — Garmin and Suunto deliberately don't. There is no software seam to enter through.

18

Could an API allow other companies to integrate it?

Technically trivial, commercially the whole gamePublishing an API is easy. Getting Shearwater or Garmin to consume it means asking them to give up the accessory lock-in that funds the ecosystem. See the pricing research: the accessory is the business model.

19

Could one transceiver communicate with multiple divers?

Yes — Garmin already doesUp to 8 divers on a SubWave network, sharing depth, distance, tank pressure, air consumption rate and remaining time. Preset messages to 30 m; tank data within 10 m.

20

Could it eventually form an underwater mesh network?

Exists, industriallyL3Harris CUUUWi already bridges above-water mobile and SATCOM users to submerged platforms. Underwater networking is a real, funded field. At recreational-diver prices, nothing.

Business · 21–29

21

What would the actual BOM/manufacturing cost be?

Not obtainable — but boundedNo BOM data is public. The bound that matters: a retail scanning sonar is $1,975 and the cheapest professional acoustic modem is $4,500. Those are selling prices at industrial volume, and your BOM at prototype quantities will not be dramatically under them.

22

Could this realistically sell for $150–300?

No — not with sonarThe cheapest imaging sonar head on earth costs 6× your top price before housing, battery, display or transceiver. A comms-only device at that price is conceivable — but that device already exists and is called the Garmin T2.

23

Would divers actually buy another piece of hardware?

The evidence says noFewer than 42% of certified divers dive even once a year. Annual spend for an active diver is $1,500–2,500 and rising. Deepblu built its own dive computer to push its app and shut down citing "lack of paid users." Mask HUDs have existed for a decade and stayed niche on price alone.

24

Is the market large enough?

No, for a consumer accessoryThe entire dive-computer category — all brands, all hardware — is ~$250M/yr. An accessory attaching to a fraction of it, in a US market where certifications fell 35% in twenty years, is not a venture-scale market.

25

Is Garmin's lock-in actually a painful enough problem?

This is the load-bearing question, and the answer is noLock-in hurts when the locked product is overpriced. Garmin's is the cheapest underwater acoustic device in existence by roughly 15×. Divers complain about the ecosystem, but they are not being gouged — they are getting industrial technology at consumer prices because of the volume the ecosystem funds.

26

What patents/proprietary technology does Garmin have?

Partly — needs a lawyerGarmin describes SubWave as proprietary and holds filings including US10227116, "Transceiver devices and related communication and navigation methods." ⚠️ I could not open patent files to assess claim scope. This is a freedom-to-operate question and it needs an actual attorney before any build.

27

Are there existing open underwater acoustic protocols?

Yes — and it's the best news hereJANUS / NATO STANAG 4748, promulgated 24 March 2017. FH-BFSK, 3–70 kHz, works to −3 dB SNR, GPL-licensed with Matlab and C reference implementations. You do not need to invent the protocol.

28

Who else is already building small underwater sonar/mapping systems?

A mature fieldShark Marine (Navigator, MAKO DDS — navies, police, fire, commercial), Blueprint Subsea (Artemis diver-held, Oculus multibeam), Blue Robotics (Ping360 at the low end), Water Linked (DVL), plus Teledyne, Nortek, EvoLogics, Sonardyne, L3Harris. And for the science applications, photogrammetry on a consumer camera.

29

Is this realistically a startup opportunity or primarily a research project?

Research projectUnderwater SLAM papers were still being published in February 2026. The approach that works needs a quote-priced DVL. The cheapest sonar is 6× your target retail. The comms half is already solved more cheaply than you could. This is a funded robotics lab or a defence supplier, not a solo undergraduate startup.

Your biggest unresolved question

"Is diver-worn real-time sonar mapping achievable at consumer size, cost, power and resolution?"

Size: yes. Depth: yes. Range: yes. Cost: no. Real-time: not yet, anywhere. Resolution: worse than a GoPro for everything except zero visibility.

Four of six are fine. The two that fail are the two that decide it — and they fail by margins that engineering effort doesn't close: the sonar head alone is 6× your target retail price, and real-time underwater SLAM on a small platform is still generating research papers rather than products.

What I'd actually keep from these notes

  • The framing instinct was right. "An open communication layer for underwater devices" is a better position than "a cheaper T2" — you correctly spotted that platform beats accessory. The problem is that the layer is already standardised and given away, and the accessory is already cheap.
  • Zero visibility is your only defensible wedge. Everything else in the applications list belongs to photogrammetry now. If any version of this survives, it's search-and-recovery in murky water — and Shark Marine is already there.
  • The questions were the right questions. Nine of twenty-nine had answers that kill or reshape the idea, and you asked all nine yourself. That's the process working. Asking 21–29 before writing code is the whole difference between a month of research and a year of building.

The pattern across this whole research programme is now four for four: SubWave, fish ID, visibility forecasting, spearfishing regulations, and now diver sonar — every idea was already built, and the build was better funded than a student could match.

That isn't bad luck and it isn't a reason to stop. It means you're generating ideas from the same information everyone else has — public, searchable, obvious to anyone who dives. The ideas that survive come from information other people don't have, which is what fifteen conversations buy you and no amount of desk research does.
