Six million recreational divers support a quarter-billion-dollar hardware category. Twenty-five thousand commercial divers support $3.2 billion. Zero humans support a nineteen-billion-dollar robot market.

Read before trusting any figure above

The numbers are worse than uncertain

5× disagreement

Three paid research firms size the scuba equipment market for the same year at $5.18 billion, $1.10 billion and $1.87 billion. That is not a rounding difference — it means they are measuring different things and none of them says which.

Diving market sizing is dominated by firms whose business model is publishing confident numbers. Treat every dollar figure here as an order of magnitude, not a measurement.

The participation numbers are far more trustworthy than the revenue numbers, because certification agencies actually count certificates. Build your reasoning on people, not on dollars.

Four groups, four equipment sets

What they use, what they miss

Scuba divers

~6M certified · <42% active
What they use

A dive computer — Shearwater, Garmin Descent, Suunto, Oceanic, Mares — plus a logging app.

  • Subsurface — free, open source, imports from 100+ dive-computer models
  • MacDive — Apple-native, 3D profile visualisation
  • Shearwater Cloud, Divelogs, DiverLog+
  • Oceanic+ on Apple Watch Ultra
What they miss
  • Sync is chronically broken, and the complaints are specific. DiverLog+ WiFi sync failing outright; crashes on export and on the calendar view; a user who could not connect an i300c at all and solved it by abandoning the vendor app; the Oceans app returning wrong dates and times even when synced directly from the computer.
  • Shearwater Cloud is web-only — no native mobile, no offline — on a device people use on boats with no signal.
  • Data is trapped per-vendor. Subsurface supports 100+ formats precisely because no interchange standard works end to end.

Freedivers

7M+ active · +52% since 2021
What they use

Breath-hold timers running CO₂ and O₂ tables, plus freediving-mode watches.

  • STAmina Apnea Trainer — O₂ and CO₂ tables with customisation
  • Apnea Assistant, Static Apnea Trainer
  • Freedive & Apnea Trainer — 180k+ downloads

The apps handle calculations, countdowns and timing. That is the whole category.

What they miss — the most serious gap in the water
  • ~59–61 freedivers die each year (DAN, analysing 2006–2011).
  • ~73% of analysed freediving accidents were fatal. If something goes wrong, you have less than a one-in-three chance.
  • The majority of fatalities involved divers who were alone or had separated from their buddy.
  • Blackout has no warning signal. Hyperventilation removes the CO₂-driven urge to breathe, so consciousness goes with no subjective cue at all.

The only mitigation in use is social: one up, one down, and the safety diver watches for thirty seconds after surfacing — because that is when blackout is most likely.

Spearfishers

$1.2B gear market · worst solo-risk profile
What they use

Navigation and GPS, species ID, weather, and logbook apps — largely borrowed from adjacent sports rather than built for them.

Emerging hardware: smart spearguns with tracking systems, plus buoyancy aids and visibility aids marketed as safety features.

What they miss
  • Nobody can answer "can I legally take this fish, at this size, here, today?" Rules are highly fragmented across countries and regional jurisdictions — licensing, gear limits, protected-species lists, size and bag limits. The market research names this compliance complexity directly; it is not an inference.
  • DAN found spearfishing and game collecting among the most common activities at the time of fatal breath-hold accidents — and notes the sport is often solitary and that many spearos are self-taught and under-informed about the risk.
  • Of six US spearfishing deaths with an established cause: three drowning, one drowning with barotrauma, one air embolism, one cardiovascular.

Technical, commercial & subsea

25,000 divers · $3.2B services · $6.9B robots
What they use
  • V-Planner (VPM-B) — OC, SCR, CCR, trimix, heliox, bailout, lost-deco and turn pressures
  • AP Diving Projection, DiveBlendr, Divesoft planning tools
  • Subsea: ROVs, AUVs, NDT inspection rigs, and inspection-compliance platforms of the LAUTEC Q type
What they miss

Honestly — not much software. CCR trimix past 50m is genuinely hard (the diluent on the bottle isn't what you breathe, bailout volumes balloon, deco mixes multiply, the scrubber budget tightens), but the tooling is mature and no clear gap was located.

The shortage in this segment is people, not software. An ageing workforce, 4,000 replacement certifications needed a year, and offshore wind driving demand up 50%.

The one thing common to all four

You cannot be found underwater

GPS does not penetrate water. Everything in use is a workaround, and every workaround has the same hole in it.

Buddy separation is the shared failure mode. Four sports, four equipment sets, one common way people die.

It is the leading circumstance in freediving fatalities. It is what the DSMB and the Nautilus Lifeline exist to mitigate. It is what diver-to-diver ultrasonic systems are built for. Nobody has solved it — and that is a signal about difficulty, not about opportunity.
You asked: can anything text underwater?

Yes. Garmin shipped it.

Radio is out — GPS at 1.5 GHz and Bluetooth at 2.4 GHz both die within centimetres of seawater. Acoustic is the only channel, and one company has already productised it for divers.

Garmin SubWave — Descent Mk3i paired with the Descent T2 transceiver. Preset messages only, not free text.

Why it's slow — and the number that matters

ConstraintConsequence
Sound travels at ~1,500 m/sPropagation delay is inherent. Not an engineering flaw — the speed of sound.
Low frequency = long range, tiny bandwidthCommercial modems reach beyond 10 km at under 400 bps; under 1,000 bps out to 100 km.
High frequency = high bandwidth, heavy attenuationRange collapses. This is the central tradeoff in the whole field.
Multipath and DopplerOnly 1–2 bits per symbol survive, and error-control coding cuts the effective rate further.
But at short rangeWide-bandwidth acoustics reach ~1 Mbps at up to 100 m.

At 30 metres, physics does not force a 45-second canned phrase.

Garmin is trading throughput for robustness, battery life and wrist-sized packaging — not hitting a wall imposed by nature. The gap between "45 seconds for a preset at 30 m" and "1 Mbps at 100 m" is genuine engineering headroom. It is also transducer design, DSP and power budgeting — a hardware company, with Garmin several years and a distribution network ahead. That reading is my inference from the research figures, not something a source states directly.

Could an app be the medium instead?

The sentence that kills the idea

Your phone is on the boat.

A dive computer underwater has no link to a phone. Bluetooth is 2.4 GHz radio and dies in centimetres of water, same as GPS. Every dive computer syncs to a phone only after you surface — that's a hardware constraint, not a software gap.

Underwater, the medium has to be acoustic and hardware-to-hardware, with no room for an app in the middle. On the surface an app can be the medium — but then it's a sync-and-share product, and Subsurface already reads 100+ dive computers for free, while Deepblu died building the social layer on top of exactly that.

And the universal tank transmitter?

A cross-brand standard already quietly exists — it just doesn't include everyone. Oceanic, Aqualung, Sherwood and Shearwater transmitters are the same hardware, made by Pelagic Pressure Systems. Shearwater's own guidance: any transmitter marked FCC ID MH8A works with a Shearwater computer, whatever the brand on the box.

Garmin and Suunto are the genuine holdouts — proprietary protocols, and transmitters that need unique air spools with flow restrictors, so the lock-in is mechanical as well as digital.

This is a business problem wearing a technical costume.

The pairing lives in the radio protocol inside the transmitter and the receiver chip — there is no software layer to insert yourself into. There's no universal standard not because nobody thought of it, but because a transmitter is a $200–350 accessory that locks a customer into a $700–1,600 computer, and the lock-in is the point.

The one software-shaped thing left here

SubWave created a networked-diver data stream that did not exist before — up to eight divers sharing depth, distance, tank pressure, air-consumption rate and remaining dive time, live. That is a genuinely new dataset about how groups of divers behave together.

Dive operators and instructors are the plausible buyer: someone responsible for eight people at once who currently has telemetry on none of them.

⚠️ Check this before spending an hour on it: whether Garmin exposes SubWave data to third parties at all. Egress was blocked, so the developer documentation could not be opened. If the answer is no, this closes too.

Five of six shipped or half-shipped, and the one that didn't is blocked by the speed of radio in seawater. This is the strongest argument on the page for talking to people instead of reading what they wrote. Community chatter lags product by years — a diver complaining about mask HUDs in a video may simply not know the NERD 2 has existed for a decade, or may be complaining about its price, which is a completely different problem to solve.

Where that leaves the two "partials"

Both are business problems, not technical ones. The gas analyzer stays separate because a consumable sensor doesn't belong inside a sealed pressure accessory. The transmitter standard stays fragmented because the accessory is the lock-in.

Neither is fixed by a better idea. Both would be fixed by a manufacturer deciding to give something up — which is exactly the kind of problem an outsider cannot solve.

What they actually want fixed

Not features. Friction.

If the feature wishlist is answered, what is the real complaint? The industry has documented its own problem in detail, and it is not on any dive computer.

The five named causes

  • The confidence gap. "Training that does not adequately qualify them to get out there and enjoy diving on a regular basis. At the core of this problem is confidence, or more to the point, its absence in many newly certified divers." People finish certified but not competent enough to organise a dive alone.
  • Lifestyle friction — the strongest of the five. "Scuba cannot be done on a whim after work. Equipment, travel, refresher training, boat fees. None are unreasonable alone, but together they form a recurring threshold. Divers who do not integrate diving into a predictable routine often perceive each return as 'starting over,' financially and mentally."
  • No shown progression. "Once certified, they weren't shown where or how to dive next. Certification was treated like a finish line instead of a starting line."
  • Low customer satisfaction with operators — named directly as a dropout driver. The qualitative complaints: rental gear running out, defective kit with unhelpful staff and no tools on hand, hidden fees, inconsistent pricing between customers on the same boat, crowded classes, being rushed, and condescending or sexist treatment.
  • Skills decay faster than most people dive. Skills degrade measurably after as little as six months of inactivity, and PADI recommends a refresher for anyone who hasn't dived in six months — roughly two hours of knowledge review, up to six hours total, plus pool time with an instructor.

Fewer than 42% of certified divers dive even once a year. Skills decay at six months. The majority of certified divers are permanently in refresher-owing status.

Every return costs money, a day, and an admission that you're rusty. That is the "starting over" feeling, precisely — and it is self-reinforcing. The longer you're out, the more it costs to come back, so the less likely you are to.

And the obvious answers are already taken

Buddy matching ships from at least three vendors: Dive Buddy (verified-ID buddy search, zero-fee dive-centre booking, site conditions), Buddy: Social Dive App (Buddy Radar matching at $2.99/month or $29.99/year, free dive log and gear management), and Dive with Buddy. ⚠️ No usage or revenue data was obtainable — and given Deepblu, assume paid conversion is poor until shown otherwise. Refresher is an agency product — PADI ReActivate, DAN's skills refresher — sold as a course, delivered by a shop, priced as a lesson.

The honest verdict

The biggest problem in recreational diving is retention, and it does not have a software answer a solo student can sell.

  • The causes are service problems, not information problems. Confidence comes from supervised water time. No app makes someone competent enough to organise a dive alone.
  • The delivery mechanism is the dive shop, and shops already buy management software at $199/month and up with agency endorsement as the moat. Retention tooling — lapsed-diver detection, six-month refresher triggers, filling empty boat seats — is a feature for those incumbents, not a company.
  • The obvious consumer answers are taken and probably not paying. Buddy matching ships from three vendors; social logging killed Deepblu after ten years.

Stated plainly rather than worked around, because inventing an answer here would be the expensive kind of wrong.

The one thing worth taking from this section regardless

The wishlist was five-sixths obsolete, and that only became visible by checking each item against what is purchasable. Forum chatter, video comments and "top 10 things users want" listicles describe a market that existed several years ago.

This is the strongest argument yet for the interview method: ask what people did last time, not what they wish existed. What people wish for is often already on a shelf they haven't looked at — or is priced at $1,650, and the real complaint is the price.

Already tried, already dead

Deepblu ran your first idea for ten years

"The Strava of diving"

A social network for divers, launched to acclaim as the platform for "the next generation of divers." It went as far as launching its own dive computer — the Cosmiq+ — purely as a means of pushing the app.

In December 2023 it took its servers offline, citing "lack of paid users." Too few would pay the subscription; server costs became unaffordable. Users had to export their own data or lose it.

A consumer dive social and logging app was run as a real business, with its own hardware, for a decade — and died of exactly what kills consumer subscription apps: people who dive six days a year will not pay every month.

The best idea in diving software, and it isn't a feature

The Oceanic+ day pass

Huish Outdoors, built with Apple, turns an Apple Watch Ultra into a dive computer to 40m. Free to download with depth, timers and basic logging; subscription unlocks decompression tracking, tissue loading, the location planner and unlimited logbook.

The day pass is the direct answer to what killed Deepblu.

Recreational divers dive a handful of days a year, on holiday. An annual subscription asks someone who dives six days a year to pay for 365; a day pass charges them on the days they are actually in the water. Price any consumer dive product this way — and note that Apple and a major manufacturer got there first, which tells you how the economics really work.

The B2B layer is full too. Dive-centre management software is an established category with agency endorsement as the moat: Dive Shop 360 (all-in-one POS built by industry people), EVE Diving (PADI-endorsed), Bloowatch (PADI and SSI integration, startup plan $199/month), Divery, Dive Admin, Diversdesk. Same shape as the restaurant research — an entrenched category at mid-hundreds monthly pricing.

What could actually be built and sold

1

★ Best fit — a data problem, not an ML problem

Spearfishing regulation compliance

"Can I legally take this fish, at this size, in this water, today?"

  • The problem is named in the market research itself as a genuine compliance burden — you are not inferring it.
  • The data is public record — state and national fisheries regulations. The hard part is ingesting, normalising and versioning hundreds of fragmented jurisdictions. That is statistics and data engineering, which is your degree.
  • Low liability framed as reference rather than advice, and no safety-critical failure mode.
  • Growing segment, least served by existing apps, and the compliance data becomes the moat because it needs perpetual maintenance.

Against it: the market is small, spearfishers are famously unwilling to pay, and that perpetual maintenance is also a perpetual cost. Run the fifteen-interview treatment on spearos before writing code — the same instrument, pointed at a different sport.

2

Best problem on the page — worst fit for you this year

Freediver blackout & buddy-loss detection

The highest human stakes in the file, in the fastest-growing segment, and only research prototypes exist: a wearable SpO₂ concept with an acoustic "latest point of return" warning (Augmented Humans, 2020); a student MAX30100 pulse-oximetry experiment; and US Patent 7,988,511, a freediving safety apparatus that auto-inflates flotation if the diver exceeds a personal time or depth limit. No mainstream commercial product was found.

Against it: it is hardware; it is a safety-critical device where a false negative kills someone; it carries regulatory and liability exposure. And there is likely a sensor-physics blocker — the mammalian dive reflex causes peripheral vasoconstriction, and wrist or finger pulse oximetry depends on peripheral perfusion, so the response that makes breath-hold diving possible may be the same one that breaks the obvious sensor. That last point is my inference, not a sourced finding — verify it with a diving-medicine source before believing me. This is a funded hardware startup or a PhD group, not a solo undergraduate project this academic year.

3

Real money, no access

Commercial dive & inspection compliance

Mandated record-keeping, real budgets, an ageing workforce, and offshore wind driving a 50% rise in subsea inspection demand with ~28% of foundations needing diver-supported work.

Against it: you cannot sell to offshore contractors without industry access, and that takes years to acquire. It is a genuinely good business for someone who already works in it.

4

Where the money is, hardest to enter

Dive travel

$5.5–6.25B growing ~11.5% — the richest consumer pool on the page. Average trip spend $1,950, rising to $2,680 for ages 51–65 and $2,840 for 65+. Liveaboards take ~18% of dive-travel revenue on just 6.3% of trips, at $250–450 a day standard and $500–1,200 luxury.

Against it: it is a marketplace business with a brutal cold-start problem, competing against established operators with existing supply relationships.

✕

Do not enter

Consumer dive log, social network, fish ID, or visibility forecast

Deepblu ran the first for ten years with its own hardware and died of it. Six companies ship the third. Five ship the fourth. Every one of these is the idea you get after one dive trip, and every one of them has already been had.

Source quality & confidence

External fetching was blocked for this entire research programme. Every figure is a search-index summary; no DAN Annual Diving Report PDF, filed financial, App Store page or academic paper was opened directly.

High confidence: the DAN freediving fatality figures and the alone-or-separated finding · the US certification decline · freediving participation growth · Deepblu's shutdown and its stated reason · Oceanic+ pricing · the fish-ID and visibility-forecast competitor lists · Nautilus Lifeline specs and price · commercial diver workforce demographics.

Low confidence — order of magnitude only: every market-size dollar figure, and especially scuba equipment, where three firms disagree by 5×. Also the active-diver population, where sources contradict each other outright.

Flagged as inference, not finding: the peripheral-vasoconstriction explanation for why underwater pulse oximetry has never shipped.

The most important number that does not exist publicly: what share of divers pay for any dive app at all. Deepblu's death suggests the answer is "very few" — and finding out for real is what fifteen conversations on a dive boat would buy you.
