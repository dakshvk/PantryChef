Idea #1 · You asked exactly the right question

It's neither a new protocol nor cheaper hardware. Both exist.

Your own framing — "is the opportunity a new protocol, or inexpensive hardware plus software that makes existing standards interoperable?" — is the correct question. The answer is that somebody already did the second one, published it, and gave it away.

The open cheap modem exists · TU Hamburg

ahoi — open-source acoustic modem, ~€600 a unit

Unit cost~€600 — €200 PCBs and components, €400 hydrophone
Size50 × 50 × 25 mm, three stacked PCBs
Range150 m and more
Bitrate260 bit/s max (Hamming encoded)
BandFSK, 25 kHz bandwidth centred at 62.5 kHz
RangingBuilt in — no extra hardware

Its authors state the motivation in one line: "Commercial underwater acoustic devices are prohibitively expensive and typically closed-source, hampering their application in affordable products and research." That is your thesis, written by researchers who then built the thing.

And it isn't alone — Coralcon is an open-source low-cost modem for underwater IoT, and Project Sabine is a DIY modem built from commercial off-the-shelf components.

An open protocol, an open 50-millimetre modem at €600, and open ranging — all published, all free to use. No product came out of any of it.

JANUS has been a NATO standard since 2017. ahoi has been published since 2020. If cheap open hardware plus an open standard were the missing pieces, the universal underwater transmitter would already exist. The bottleneck was never the protocol and was never the component cost.

What the bottleneck actually is

A universal transceiver is worth exactly as much as the number of devices willing to speak to it — and the devices that matter won't.

  • Dive computers won't, because the pairing lives in the receiver chip and the accessory lock-in is the business model.
  • Garmin calls SubWave proprietary and is extending it, not opening it — the Descent S1 Buoy shipped this cycle.
  • So you'd ship a universal device whose only conversation partner is another copy of itself.

And note the price comparison that keeps recurring: ahoi costs ~€600 open and research-grade; Garmin's T2 costs $200–350 closed and consumer-grade. The open version is more expensive. Volume beats openness on price, every time.

Where the idea does survive — and it isn't divers

Your device list quietly contains the answer. ROVs, AUVs, scientific sensors, environmental sensors and surface buoys are already open — they run ROS, they publish interfaces, nobody is defending an accessory margin. That's the marine-robotics and research market, where interoperability is a genuine want. It is also exactly the market ahoi and EvoLogics already serve, and it is small. Real, but not a consumer business.

Idea #2 · The strongest idea in this programme so far

The AR fish-tracking HUD

This is the first idea where the answer isn't "someone already built it." It's also the first with a regulatory risk that could remove the market during development. Three findings, in order of how much they matter.

Finding 1 · The biggest risk, and it is happening right now

Forward-facing sonar is being banned across fishing

  • Two major bass fishing circuits have banned or restricted live sonar.
  • Twelve states are moving to ban or restrict it on state fisheries.
  • Wisconsin's DNR took a proposed ban on forward-facing and 360-degree imaging to its spring hearings.
  • A major industry figure has argued it should be illegal recreationally, not just in tournaments.

The stated reasoning is precisely what your product would trigger: the technology "has increased the pressure on fish populations by making anglers more efficient in locating and catching fish," and it "blurs the hard-to-define boundary between fair chase and exploitation."

And spearfishing's fair-chase norms are stricter than angling's, not looser. Hawaii already bans scuba for spearfishing explicitly to promote fair chase. Florida bans powerheads, bangsticks and rebreathers for harvest. You would be building the most regulated-against technology in fishing, for the sport with the strongest fair-chase culture.

Your instinct to position it as "underwater situational awareness" rather than a spearfishing HUD isn't just better marketing. It may be survival.

Search and rescue, public-safety diving, commercial inspection, scientific diving, cave and wreck diving, low-visibility operations — none of those buyers can have the product legislated away from them. A spearfishing-first product can be, in your own state, while you're still building it.

Finding 2 · Sonar gives you five of your nine fields

Fisheries acoustics is a mature science and split-beam echosounders do exactly this job — so the question isn't whether sonar can see fish. It's which of the readouts in your mock-up survive contact with the physics.

Your mock readout
FISH 7.2 m
18° left
0.8 m below
Moving right

Everything in this box is achievable. Range, bearing, relative depth and direction of travel are geometry — they fall out of the echo's timing and the beam's angle, and they're what a split-beam system is built to measure.

The four fields that aren't in the box are the hard ones — and they're the ones that would make the product feel magic: species, approximate size, confidence, and predicted trajectory.

Fish size from acoustics uses TS = 20 log(L) + b20, where b20 is species-specific. You need the species to get the size — and size is one of your cues for species.
Field in your HUDVerdictWhy
Fish detectedYesCore function of every echosounder
DistanceYesEcho timing. The easiest measurement in acoustics.
Bearing / angleYesWhat the "split" in split-beam is for
Relative depthYesGeometry from bearing plus your own depth sensor
Direction of movementYesTrack a target across successive pings
SpeciesNoFisheries science lists "difficulty in fish species identification" among its own unsolved problems. Not a resolution issue — an information-theoretic one.
Approximate sizeUnreliableNeeds the species-specific constant and the fish's tilt angle. Target strength varies strongly with orientation — the same fish reads very differently as it turns.
ConfidenceOnly as good as the aboveA confidence score on an unreliable species estimate is a confident-looking guess
Predicted trajectoryHardFish don't move ballistically. Short-horizon extrapolation, at best.
And your architecture instinct is right

Camera in clear water, sonar in murk, AI fusing them — that is the correct design, and it plays to each sensor's actual strength. Real-time fish detection and species classification from imagery is well-established and improving fast; it's the sonar half that can't do species. So the fusion isn't a nice-to-have, it's the only way to fill the species field at all.

One catch worth knowing: split-beam sizing needs compound transducer array geometry with accurate calibration. That is not a $1,975 Ping360 — it's a purpose-built array, and it's the part of the bill of materials you can't shop for off a shelf.

Finding 3 · A student team is building the display right now

AREX AR dive mask — 2026 James Dyson Award

An AR dive mask with a built-in HUD and dive computer, projecting hands-free life-support data into the diver's vision, with an RGB LED for buddy identification. Prototypes tested to 45 metres.

Their documented problems are the ones you would inherit, and reading them is worth more than any market report:

  • Early prototypes struggled with glare and narrow viewing angles — the core optical challenge is delivering high-contrast data without obstructing the natural view
  • Months in the lab on lens positioning and field-of-view calibration just to stabilise the optical engine
  • ⭐ Touchscreens fail underwater, so they prototyped mechanical dials that can be operated blind, in thick technical gloves
  • Pressure and waterproofing across depth on top of all of it

The US Navy also fields an AR diving helmet, and the existing commercial HUDs — Shearwater NERD 2 at $1,650, Scubapro Galileo HUD, Halcyon Symbios — remain stuck with technical and rebreather divers on price and on the fogging problem.

The display half is being solved by people you could email this week.

AREX is a student project. So is Vimicx — the 2026 New Venture Competition finalist turning 2D sonar into real-time 3D underwater AR, in your own competition. Between them they are building both halves of your idea, and neither is a competitor the way Garmin is. They are the two most valuable conversations available to you, and they cost nothing.
Where this leaves you

One idea closes, one opens

Idea #1 — closed, and instructively so

The "USB-C for underwater" instinct was right, and that is precisely why it already exists twice: an open NATO protocol since 2017 and an open €600 modem since 2020. Neither produced a product, because the barrier is that the devices worth talking to are commercially unwilling to talk. Worth understanding before building anything else in this space — it's the same wall the transmitter-interoperability idea hit.

Idea #2 — the first one that survives

The hard part genuinely isn't "someone already built it." What it needs is:

  • Position it as situational awareness from day one, not a spearfishing product — for regulatory survival, not marketing polish
  • Design the HUD around the five fields sonar can deliver, and let the camera carry species when the water allows. Don't promise a species field that fails in the exact conditions sonar exists to handle.
  • Talk to AREX and Vimicx before writing a line of code
  • Ask spearos the fair-chase question directly. Not "would you use this" — "should this be legal?" If the sport's own culture rejects it, you'll learn that from fifteen conversations rather than from a state legislature three years in.

That last one is the whole interview method pointed at the single question that decides the market — and it's a question only spearfishers can answer.
