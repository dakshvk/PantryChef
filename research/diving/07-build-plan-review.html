Prior art #1 · Better news than you had

Open Echo is real, and it drives fish-finder transducers

Neumi/open_echo — Jan Neumann

An Arduino-compatible open-source echo sounder. The TUSS4470 shield stacks directly on an Arduino Uno, with RAW-data firmware covering 40 kHz to 1 MHz, in air or water. A Python backend visualises the return signal, changes settings, and streams NMEA.

Two details that matter more than the rest:

  • ⭐ It has been tested on a wide range of transducers and is compatible with all of them — from car parking sensors to Lowrance TripleShot side-scan heads. You do not have to fabricate a transducer. You can drive a commercial fish-finder head.
  • The project began by reverse-engineering a cheap commercial fish finder, which is exactly the shortcut you were looking for and someone has already taken it.

So your $100–500 target for a single-channel echo sounder is plausible, and the path is documented. That part of the plan survives intact.

This is simultaneously the best and the most sobering news in the file.

Best: undergraduates did it, it's open source, and you can start from their design instead of a blank page. Their front-end trick — microcontrollers and analog pulse-shaping instead of a proper multichannel ADC — is exactly the cost-reduction you were looking for, already invented.

Sobering: $1,710, not $100–500. And the demonstrated result is a static object at 1.2 metres, in a tank — against your target of 2–15 m in open water on a swimming human.

One bookkeeping note: you list the Ping360 at $2,750; the figure I found was $1,975. Worth checking which configuration you're pricing — because at $1,975 versus Harvey Mudd's $1,710, building your own array saves roughly 13%, not 90%. The cost advantage of DIY here is real but small; the advantage that matters is architectural control, not price.

The constraint that decides your design

Four to eight transducers can't give you 2–5°

Your plan asks for 4–8 transducers in one place and 2–5° angular resolution in another. Those two numbers are inconsistent, and the relationship between them is fixed by physics, not by engineering effort.

// Element spacing is set by sidelobe suppression
spacing d ≈ 0.7 λ — tighter wastes elements, wider creates grating lobes

// Half-power beamwidth of a uniform linear array, broadside
θ ≈ 0.886 · λ / (N · d)
substituting d = 0.7λ, the wavelength cancels:
θ ≈ 0.886 / (0.7 · N) radians ≈ 72 / N degrees
N = 8 → ~9°
N = 16 → ~4.5°
N = 32 → ~2.3°

⚠️ That derivation is mine, from the 0.7λ spacing rule plus standard uniform-linear-array theory — not lifted from a source. Check it before you build to it. But the shape of the answer is not in doubt: angular resolution is set by element count, and eight elements buys you about nine degrees.

And here's the part that helps you

Because the wavelength cancels, frequency doesn't change your angular resolution at all — it only changes how physically big the array is. Which means going high-frequency is close to free for your use case:

Frequencyλ in water16-element aperture
200 kHz7.5 mm~84 mm
450 kHz3.3 mm~37 mm
700 kHz2.1 mm~24 mm

Higher frequency normally costs you range through absorption — but your range target is 2–15 m, where that barely matters. So the short-range decision you already made buys you a physically tiny array for free. A 16-element, ~4.5° array at 450 kHz is under four centimetres wide. That is wearable, and it's a genuinely good consequence of your own architecture choice.

Where the money actually goes — and it isn't transducers

The TUSS4470 is a single-channel driver. Beamforming needs N receive channels sampled phase-coherently — every element digitised simultaneously, with known relative timing. That is the difference between an echo sounder and an imaging array, and it's where Harvey Mudd's $1,710 went.

It's also why the hobbyist literature keeps pointing at DSP or FPGA-class hardware for real imaging. Steal Harvey Mudd's answer — low-cost microcontrollers plus analog pulse-shaping front ends — rather than rediscovering why a 16-channel coherent ADC is expensive.

What I'd change

The plan, adjusted

Keep

Phase 0, unchanged, starting now

Public datasets, open drivers, laptop. Answer the DVL-replacement question before anything else exists. This is still the single highest-value thing you can do and it remains free.

Adjust

Phase 1 — single channel, not an array

Open Echo plus a commercial fish-finder transducer, tethered to a laptop. $100–500 is realistic here. The goal is one honest question: does cheap acoustic hardware return geometry you can actually match between scans? Don't build an array until single-channel data has proven the algorithm.

Adjust

Phase 2 — budget $1,700, not $500, and start from HMC's design

Their platform is open and built to be modified. Forking a published, characterised array beats designing one, and it converts your hardest phase into an integration problem. Target 16 elements at 450 kHz for ~4.5° in a 37 mm aperture.

Fix

The resolution target

Pick one: 8 elements at ~9°, or 2–5° at 16–32 elements. You can't have both. And 9° may genuinely be fine for obstacle awareness — decide what the application needs before deciding the element count.

Fix

The range expectation

Harvey Mudd demonstrated 1.2 m on a static target in a tank. Your target is 2–15 m in open water on a moving diver. That gap is the real experiment, and it's driven by transmit power and receiver sensitivity, not by cleverness. Treat 2 m as the first milestone, not 15.

Add

An Experiment 0.5 nobody has run

Your list is good but skips the thing most likely to kill it late: bubbles. A diver exhales a plume of near-perfect acoustic reflectors directly beside the sensor, every few seconds. No source I found measures this for a diver-worn array. You could test it in a pool with Open Echo and a snorkel for under $200 — and if it's fatal, you'd want to know before Phase 2, not after.

That's a real outcome, and it's reachable from a dorm room.
