01 · The disorganisation, fixed

There is one project here, and one demo

The project

Can a small forward-looking acoustic array plus a cheap IMU plus sonar scan matching give a swimming human enough spatial and motion information to localise and map — without a DVL and without a $2,000 commercial sonar?

That is a single technical question. Everything else — the HUD, fish tracking, search and rescue, cave navigation, inspection — is an application layered on top of it, and none of them matter if the answer is no.

You were treating these as two ideas. They're one sensor problem and a list of display problems.

The sonar mapping project and the fish-tracking HUD run on identical hardware: acoustic array, IMU, embedded compute, and a way to show a human what it found. The only difference is what you point it at and what you draw on the screen. Solve perception once; the applications are software.

03 · The honest ledger

What's proven, and what nobody has done

Every capability you need has been demonstrated. Just never all at once, and never on a swimming human.

Proven — you can start from these

  • Real-time tracking of individual moving fish — ARIS/DIDSON, commercially, ~7 fps at 12 m, with length, depth, distance and swim direction
  • Diver-scale form factor — ARIS Explorer 3000 is 2 kg in water, 26 cm, 15 W
  • The array architecture — 128 beams, 30° forward fan, 1.8 MHz/15 m and 3.0 MHz/5 m modes. You reasoned to this independently.
  • Cheap acoustic electronics — Open Echo drives commercial fish-finder transducers from an Arduino
  • A buildable open array — Harvey Mudd, $1,710, published and designed to be modified
  • Motion-distortion correction for scanning sonar — EKF undistortion, modified-FastSLAM, RBPF with sliding-window scan modules
  • The display — AREX AR dive mask, tested to 45 m
  • Fish detection by neural net on acoustic-camera data — published, multi-species

The gap — nobody has done this

  • Any of it from a swimming diver. Every acoustic-camera deployment is a tripod, weir, mount or ROV. ROVs hover; divers don't.
  • Motion estimation without a DVL. Every published undistortion method uses DVL velocity. Scan-matching-only motion estimation exists in the literature but has not been tested at diver speeds or diver motion profiles.
  • Exhaust bubbles. A diver vents a plume of near-perfect acoustic reflectors beside the sensor every few seconds. No measurement of this exists that I could find.
  • Cost. 128 phase-coherent receive channels at 3 MHz is the wall between a $1,710 array and an ARIS.
  • Multi-diver interference. Two people with active sonar in the same band, unstudied at this scale.

The unclaimed thing is the join: perception from a moving diver, at a price that isn't a defence budget.

That is a much better-defined research question than anything earlier in this programme, and every one of the eight proven lines is a component you start from rather than invent.

The design constraint that saves you money

Element spacing is pinned near 0.7 λ, and beamwidth for a uniform linear array works out to roughly 72 / N degrees — so 8 elements gives ~9°, 16 gives ~4.5°, 32 gives ~2.3°. Resolution is bought with element count, not with money or frequency.

Because the wavelength cancels, frequency only sets physical size — and since your range target is short, you can go high-frequency for free. At 450 kHz a 16-element array is ~37 mm wide. ⚠️ That derivation is mine from standard array theory — verify before building to it.

The market inverts against the product. The people who most want it pay least; the people who'd pay properly number in the thousands.

That's the same shape as everything else in this research programme — the money gets deeper as the people get fewer. It doesn't kill the project, but it does decide the order: build for public safety and science first, where a few thousand dollars is a normal purchase order, and let recreational use arrive when the hardware cost has fallen by an order of magnitude.

What that means numerically, being blunt

At a plausible early price of $5,000 into public-safety and scientific diving, capturing even 200 units is $1M — a real business for one person, and nowhere near venture scale. At a recreational price of $400 you would need 2,500 units for the same revenue, from buyers who currently spend $300–600 on their entire kit.

Neither of those is a reason not to run Phase 0. They're the reason not to design a consumer product before Phase 3.

07 · The other idea, put in its place

Spearfishing is the demo, not the business

Your own conclusion, and the research supports it for reasons beyond the regulatory one.

ArgumentStatus
Is it currently illegal?No. No electronic-aid restriction was found in California, Florida or Hawaii spearfishing rules. The adopted 2026 forward-facing-sonar bans are private tournament rules (MLF, B.A.S.S., Outlaw Outdoors), not law.
Could it become illegal?The template is already written. Minnesota prohibits artificial lights "to lure or attract fish — or to see fish in the water — while spearing." Change one noun.
Does regulation distinguish find / track / target?No. Every rule found bans a device or method — powerhead, bangstick, rebreather, scuba, artificial light — not a capability.
The commercial argument, which is strongerThey can't pay. Even in Hawaii — the densest spearfishing market in the US — that's ~90,000 frequent participants whose whole kit costs less than your prototype.
The product argument, which is subtlerAt the moment of the shot you are looking at the fish. Your eyes and a camera both beat sonar at 2 m in clear water. Sonar's value is the 5–15 m approach — so "aim assist" is the least defensible framing of a genuinely useful capability.

Keep it as the demo. Tracking a moving fish is the most visceral possible proof that the perception stack works — far more legible in thirty seconds than a wall-following map. Show it; don't sell it. And note the useful accident: a system that can track a fish can track a diver, a target, a piece of debris or a structure. The hard part is the tracker, and the tracker is application-neutral.
