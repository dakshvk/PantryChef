Part 1 · Your core technical question

Can a small forward-looking sonar continuously track moving fish?

Yes — and it's been doing it commercially for two decades. The instrument is the acoustic camera: DIDSON, and its current generation, the Sound Metrics ARIS.

And the tracking performance is exactly what you described wanting. DIDSON runs seven frames per second at 12 m range, producing near video-quality imagery through time. It yields quantifiable fish length, depth and distance from the camera. Technicians can distinguish fish swimming side-by-side from head-to-tail, and determine which direction each fish is swimming. ARIS resolves details as small as a few millimetres out to 40 m, and there is already a published deep neural network doing multi-species fish detection across multiple acoustic cameras.

Two kilograms in water. Twenty-six centimetres. Fifteen watts. Divers routinely carry bigger lights than that.

The "shrink it until a diver can wear it" problem you were bracing for has largely been solved by somebody else. This is not a lab rig on a cart — it is already, physically, a diver-carryable instrument.
Worth noticing about your own reasoning

You independently arrived at their architecture

Last round you reasoned toward a narrow forward-looking fan, very high frequency, many elements, short range — from first principles, working from the physics. That is precisely what Sound Metrics ships: a 30° horizontal fan, 128 beams, 1.8–3.0 MHz, and a deliberate 15 m / 5 m range split between detection and identification.

It also confirms the array math. At 3 MHz, λ in water is 0.5 mm, so 0.7λ spacing is 0.35 mm — 128 elements is a 45 mm aperture. Under five centimetres. And 72/N gives ~0.56° beamwidth. That's how they fit millimetre resolution into a housing the size of a lunchbox, and it's the design you were already converging on.

What is still not solved

  • Every deployment is stationary. ARIS and DIDSON sit on tripods, weirs, river mounts and ROVs — counting salmon escapement, monitoring restored wetlands, observing fish around hydroelectric facilities. Nobody swims with one. The moving-platform problem is untouched.
  • 128 phase-coherent receive channels at 3 MHz is the wall between Harvey Mudd's $1,710 array and this. Pricing is quote-only ⚠️ — I could not obtain a figure, but this class of instrument is not consumer-priced.
  • 0.7 m minimum range. Fine for tracking; awkward for a device worn on your body.

So the honest answer: the sensor exists, works, tracks individual moving fish with length and heading in real time, and is already diver-sized. The two open problems are cost and diver motion. Neither is a physics wall. Both are hard.

One product note on "the right aim"

A speargun shot happens at a few metres. ARIS's identification mode reaches 5 m and its minimum range is 0.7 m, so the geometry works. But think about when the information is worth most.

At the moment of the shot, in the water clarity where spearfishing actually happens, you are looking directly at the fish. Your eyes and the camera both beat sonar there. The sonar's real value is in the 5–15 m approach — knowing something is there, how big, which way it's moving, before you can see it. Aim assist is the demo. Approach awareness is the product.

Part 2 · You were right to push

The regulations, jurisdiction by jurisdiction

You said this needed researching rather than assuming "sonar is banned." You were right, and I overstated it last time.

Correction
What I said: "Twelve states are moving to ban or restrict it on state fisheries."
What the evidence actually supports: twelve state agencies were reported to be in agreement with a proposal, discussed at an annual consortium of fish and wildlife resource officers. Separately, Wisconsin's DNR asked anglers whether they would support a ban. Neither is adopted law, and I could not confirm that any state has adopted a recreational forward-facing sonar restriction.

That's a meaningful difference. "Twelve states are banning it" and "officers at a conference agreed a proposal was worth pursuing" are not the same fact, and the second one shouldn't change what you build.

What is actually adopted for 2026 — and it's all private tournament rules

And for spearfishing specifically — nothing about electronics

California and Florida spearfishing rules regulate locations, species and gear — powerheads, bangsticks, rebreathers, and Hawaii's scuba prohibition. No electronic-aid restriction was found in any of them.

The one real precedent, and it's the template

Minnesota prohibits using artificial lights to lure or attract fish — or to see fish in the water — while spearing.

That is a sensory-aid ban written specifically for spearing. In plain language, an AR sonar HUD is a device to see fish in the water while spearing. If a regulator wanted to prohibit your product, that sentence already exists and they would only have to change one noun.

Your find / track / target distinction — honest answer

Current regulation doesn't make it. Every rule found bans a device or a method — powerhead, bangstick, rebreather, scuba, artificial light — not a capability. A device that finds, tracks and aims is regulated as one device.

The one hint in your favour: the forward-facing sonar debate is argued in terms of locating efficiency — the technology "has increased the pressure on fish populations by making anglers more efficient in locating and catching fish." That suggests regulators care about finding rather than aiming. ⚠️ But that's my read of the pattern, not a documented distinction, and I would not build a strategy on it.

A classifier tells you what something is. A tracker tells you where it will be. Those are different products built on different mathematics.

Which upgrades AREX from "competitor who got there first" to "the team that already solved the half of the problem you least want to solve." Their documented pain — months of lens positioning and FOV calibration, glare, mechanical dials because touchscreens fail underwater — is a year of work you would otherwise repeat. Email them.
Where this leaves it

The thesis narrows, and gets better

Every capability you want has been demonstrated — just never all at once, and never on a swimming human.

  • Real-time tracking of individual moving fish with length and heading — ARIS, commercially, for years
  • Diver-scale size, weight and power — ARIS, already
  • The array architecture and physics — ARIS, and you reasoned to it independently
  • Cheap acoustic electronics — Open Echo
  • An open, buildable array — Harvey Mudd, $1,710
  • Motion-distortion correction — published, but leaning on a DVL
  • The display — AREX, this year

The unclaimed thing is the join: perception from a moving diver, at a price that isn't a defence budget. That is a narrower and much better-defined research question than where you started, and every one of those seven lines is a component you can start from rather than invent.

And your own conclusion stands, now better supported

Don't make spearfishing the wedge. Not because it's banned — it isn't, today, anywhere I could find — but because the capability that makes it exciting is the capability that invites the ban, the template for that ban is already written in Minnesota, and the sport's own fair-chase culture is stricter than angling's. Search and rescue, low-visibility navigation and inspection buy the same technology with none of that exposure. Spearfishing is the demo you show. It isn't the company you build.
