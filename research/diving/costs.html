Part 1 · I have to reverse my own recommendation

The spearfishing regulation idea already exists — free, four times over

Last round I ranked this #1, best fit for you. Researched properly, it doesn't hold. The exact product — "can I legally take this fish, at this size, here, today?" — is called Fish Rules, and it has been shipping across the entire US coastline for years.

The complexity was real — it's just already handled

Worth recording, because it shows how hard the domain genuinely is:

  • Species × method × jurisdiction. In Florida state waters Permit and African Pompano are hook-and-line only — but spearing them is legal in federal waters. Same species, same day, same gear, different side of a line you cannot see.
  • Method bans by state. Hawaii prohibits scuba for spearfishing entirely, on fair-chase grounds. California permits both scuba and breath-hold. Florida bans harvest by rebreather for everything except lionfish.
  • Species carve-outs. California prohibits spearing giant (black) sea bass, garibaldi, gulf grouper, broomtail grouper, trout, salmon and broadbill.
  • Geography. MPAs, the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, and a specific ban in the Upper Keys from Long Key to Key Largo.
  • Even transit is regulated. You may cross an MPA that bans spearfishing with catch aboard only if your gear is unloaded and not in hand and you stay at the surface — no stopping, anchoring or entering the water.

And unlike the restaurant mandates, the penalties have teeth

  • Florida: $50–$500 per fish for a first undersized-fish offence. Multiple fish or protected species can be a misdemeanour with up to 60 days in jail. Spearfishing violations are a Level Two violation.
  • California: fines exceeding $1,000, misdemeanour charges, potential jail.
  • A documented federal case: two years' probation and a $2,500 criminal fine for illegal spearfishing in Florida.

So the pain is real and the forcing function is real. The problem is only that someone already solved it and gave it away.

The one residual gap — and why it's still not a company

⚠️ I could not verify whether Fish Rules filters by gear method. If it shows hook-and-line rules without distinguishing spearfishing restrictions, there is a narrow wedge, because the species × method × jurisdiction matrix above is genuinely distinct for spearos.

But that is a feature request to Fish Rules, not a startup. They have the data pipeline, the coastline coverage, the offline app and the users. Adding a gear-type filter is a sprint for them and a company for you.

The method was right even though the answer was wrong.

Check whether the thing already exists before ranking it #1 — which is exactly the mistake I made last round, and exactly the mistake the diver feature wishlist made. It is the same failure twice in one research programme, which is worth more than the finding itself.
Part 2 · Your question: why is it all so expensive?

The price is engineered

Diving is not expensive because regulators are hard to manufacture. It is expensive because four interlocking mechanisms hold the price up on purpose, and each one closes an exit from the others.

1

Enforced retail pricing

  • Manufacturers set an MSRP and a MAP — minimum advertised price — beneath it.
  • Major brands do not allow authorised dealers to sell online or ship products at all. Scubapro is named specifically.
  • Dealers may discount roughly 10–15%, and no further.
  • Dealers have been threatened with losing their dealership for giving customers larger discounts than the manufacturer allowed.

The stated purpose is explicit: territories and dealer margins are protected to maintain loyalty to manufacturers and keep local dive shops viable enough to employ knowledgeable staff.

2

Parts lockdown

Aqualung, Atomic, Cressi, Scubapro and others do not sell regulator service kits or parts to consumers at all, arguing consumers have no right to repair, on liability and safety grounds.

Their argument is not frivolous — "a badly serviced regulator or a low-quality o-ring can be catastrophic at depth." It is life-support equipment.

But the effect is that the only legal path to a serviced regulator runs through an authorised dealer.

3

Warranty coupled to recurring authorised service

  • Scubapro recommends service every 24 months or 100 dives, by a certified technician at an authorised dealer.
  • Most manufacturers require annual authorised service to keep the warranty and the "free parts for life" programme.

"Free parts for life" is the elegant part of the design: the parts are free, and you pay labour every year, forever, at a shop the manufacturer controls. Stop, and you lose both.

4

The used market is booby-trapped

  • Buying secondhand voids the warranty — and with it the free-parts programme.
  • Service kits for a model may be discontinued, or the brand no longer supported. "Very old models you can't service because you can't get spare parts are common in the used market."
  • A used regulator must be overhauled before first use: $100–300+.

So the obvious escape from a $1,000–3,000 purchase leads to a device that may be unserviceable, and costs $100–300 before you can breathe off it.

The price floor exists to keep dive shops alive. Dive shops are how the industry creates divers. And the cost is a named cause of the dropout that is shrinking the sport.

The structure protecting the channel is helping drain the pool that channel fishes in. Every cost-reduction play in scuba is therefore also an attack on the training channel — which is why DGX's founder "took flak for supposedly destroying the dive industry by selling dive equipment on the internet." That's a genuine strategic tension, not a rhetorical one. Take it seriously before picking a side.

What this means for you: people are not waiting for someone to make scuba cheaper — they are leaving scuba for the thing that already is. Any cost play inside scuba swims against both a defended price structure and a migration that has already started.
1

Cheapest to build · real gap · monetises badly

A serviceability database

The problem, straight from the research: a buyer of a used regulator cannot tell whether it's a bargain or a brick. Are service kits still made? Is the brand still supported? Which independent technicians service it? What does an overhaul actually cost?

Why this one is interesting: it doesn't fight the price structure head-on — it makes the used market work, and the used market is the single biggest available lever on cost.

What's needed: model-by-model parts and support data (partly public via manuals, partly hostile to collect), a directory of independent technicians, and the discipline to keep it current. Cost: time, not capital. No safety-critical failure mode if framed as reference rather than advice.

Against it: it monetises badly. Affiliate revenue on used gear is small, and the people who need it most are the least willing to pay.

2

Proven — and not a student project

Direct-to-consumer gear

DGX Gears is the working proof. Own-brand regulators, published service manuals, and all service kits and parts sold without restriction — explicit right-to-repair, in a category where everyone else refuses.

And the industry's own analysts say the door is open: "D2C in the dive industry could be more easily implemented by a startup without an already established network of resellers, similar to how Dell disrupted the PC industry" — precisely because a startup has no dealer relationships to protect.

What's needed: capital, an OEM manufacturing relationship, product liability insurance on life-support equipment, inventory, and a tolerance for being hated by every dive shop in town. This is a funded hardware company.
3

The only one you could start this weekend

Shore-diving infrastructure

A shore dive costs a $10–20 air fill. A boat dive costs $75–150. The cheap path already exists — it's just invisible. Entries, parking, surface conditions, hazards, tides, what's actually down there, and whether you can do it without a buddy who owns a boat.

What's needed: legwork and local knowledge. Nothing else.

Against it: it overlaps the visibility-forecast wave (DiveSight, DiveViz, Marla Blue, VizFinder, Viz-app) and the buddy-matching wave (Dive Buddy, Buddy Social) — both crowded, both recent, and neither obviously making money.

✕

Won't work — don't waste a weekend

Three dead ends

  • A price-comparison site. MAP pricing means the prices are the same everywhere by design. There is nothing to compare.
  • A group-buying scheme. Dealers lose their dealership for discounting beyond the allowance.
  • Anything assuming gear is expensive because of manufacturing cost. It isn't. It's expensive because the channel is protected.
The one-line version

If you read nothing else

Spearfishing regs: already free, four times over. Don't build it.

Cost: diving is expensive on purpose, to fund the shops that create divers. The cheap alternative already exists and is growing 52% a year. And of the three ways to attack the price, the only one you could start alone is a serviceability database that makes the used market work — which is genuinely useful and probably doesn't make money.
