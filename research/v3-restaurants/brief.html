01 · Where the money actually goes

Before deciding what to build, know the size of the pool you're fishing in. A typical US independent does ~$1.2M revenue at 3–6% net. That's roughly $48,000 of profit for a year of somebody's life.

Myth killed first

False "90% of restaurants fail in year one" Traceable to nothing. H.G. Parsa (Ohio State) found no evidence for it anywhere in the literature. When American Express was asked in writing for the data behind its own 90% claim, it said it could not provide any. Actual first-year failure is ~17% — lower than the 19% rate for all other service businesses. "Restaurants are dying" is not a defensible framing for a pitch.

02 · The waste numbers don't hold up

You asked how much food restaurants waste and why. The honest answer is that nobody credibly knows, and the three figures everyone quotes are misattributed, unsourced, or vendor-supplied.

70.2% of uneaten food at restaurants is plate waste — post-consumer, food the customer was served and chose not to eat.

ReFED Insights Engine. Only ~30% of restaurant surplus ever passes through a walk-in, a prep station, or a purchase order. That's the ceiling on what inventory software can touch — before you write a line of code.
Realistic software-addressable slice: 15–20% of total restaurant food surplus.
BucketShareCan software touch it?
Plate waste — portions too large, customer didn't finish~70%No. Portion size, menu design, buffet ops.
Trim & prep loss — peels, bones, fatpart of ~30%Barely. Physically determined by yield. Better tracking improves costing accuracy, not waste volume.
Spoilage from over-orderingpart of ~30%Yes — the real target. Par levels, order forecasting, purchase-vs-usage variance.
Poor rotationpart of ~30%Partially. Software can prompt; it cannot see the walk-in.
Over-prep / overproductionpart of ~30%Partially. Needs forecasting tied to POS.
Cooking errors, comps, remakessmallNo.

Three load-bearing numbers that fail inspection

Misattr. "$162 billion lost to restaurant food waste" This is USDA ERS's 2010 estimate of total US retail + consumer food loss — two-thirds of it in homes and all away-from-home eating combined, one-third in grocery retail. Restaurants are a fraction of a fraction. Anyone quoting $162B as a restaurant number is careless or selling something.
Folklore "4–10% of food purchased never reaches a customer" Attributed variously to the National Restaurant Association and the Green Restaurant Association, appearing almost entirely in vendor and trade content. No primary study, methodology, or sample was locatable. Plausible magnitude — and it is the load-bearing number in nearly every waste-software pitch.
Biased "7:1 ROI on waste reduction" (Champions 12.3, 2018) A real study — 114 sites, 12 countries. But the sites were already running waste programmes (pure survivorship bias), the data was supplied by Winnow and Leanpath — the two companies the ratio justifies buying — there was no control group, it modelled avoided cost rather than auditing a P&L, it measured only the ~30% kitchen slice, and it is eight years old.
Vendor The cause breakdown itself ReFED's pre-consumer cause split is derived from Leanpath's aggregated customer data, and its catering plate-waste rates are "based on expert interviews" — not measurement. Leanpath sells waste-tracking hardware to institutional caterers and hotels. The cause mix in a Sodexo cafeteria is not the cause mix in a 60-seat bistro.
Holds Manual waste logging collapses in 2–3 weeks Consistent across every source. Entries get filled in from memory at end of shift rather than at disposal, and logging is the first thing skipped when service gets busy — which is exactly when overproduction happens. The dataset is systematically biased at precisely the moments that matter.

And the two authoritative sources disagree by 2×: EPA puts foodservice at ~26.5M tons (2019); ReFED at 12.5M tons (2024). University of Minnesota researchers found commonly cited food-waste estimates "inaccurate, incomplete, overstated, or contradictory."

You cannot measure waste without hardware, because measurement requires a human action during the busiest moment of their day, and they will not do it.

Every company that survived this category either added a camera (Winnow, Leanpath, Orbisk, Kitro — and Phood, which pivoted to computer vision) or added a transaction (Spoiler Alert, B2B surplus liquidation). Pure dashboards did not.
Not one of these vendors sells to independent restaurants. Every named customer is a hotel chain, cruise line, casino, contract caterer or corporate campus.
VendorPriceWho actually buys

Why enterprise only: buffets (centrally controlled, enormous visible overproduction), ESG reporting (Compass, Sodexo and Hilton have investor-facing disclosure duties — an independent restaurant has no one to report to), central procurement (one contract amortises CAC across 3,000 sites), and tender advantage in contract catering. None of those exist for a 60-seat independent.

The category's ceiling: Winnow — clear leader, 12+ years old, $36–45M raised, hardware, blue-chip customers in 94 countries — sits at roughly $26M revenue ⚠️ (self-reported aggregator data).

And nothing compels the spend

California SB 1383 covers restaurants with 250+ seats or 5,000+ sq ft — which exempts the typical single unit. It mandates diversion, donation and record-keeping, not measurement or prevention. Penalties are $50–$200 with a mandatory 60-day cure period. Nobody buys $200/month software to avoid a $100 fine they can cure.

Peer-reviewed

Science, Sept 2024 (UC San Diego Rady School): of the first five US states with food waste bans, only Massachusetts measurably reduced landfilled waste. Combined effect did not exceed 3%. The authors explicitly call for policymakers to reassess these laws as "having little to no effect." The flagship voluntary programme, the US Food Waste Pact, cut 4,000 tons against a 12.5-million-ton sector — 0.03%.

Bottom-up, $1M independent at 32% food cost
Annual purchases $320,000

That supports a $50–150/month product. It does not support $500/month on waste reduction alone — and it sits below what MarketMan already charges for something far broader.

Sell the food cost line. Never the waste line.

Nobody has waste on their list — tech spending priorities for 2026 run digital guest experience 57%, POS 53%, driving traffic 52%, reducing food costs 36%, and waste tracking does not chart as a category at all. Waste reduction is the mechanism. Margin is the pitch.

The reframe matters, because "help kitchens use inventory before it spoils and cost menus accurately" isn't a waste product — it's an inventory and COGS product, and there the evidence is favourable: food cost is named by 9 in 10 operators as a significant challenge, 42% of operators were unprofitable last year, and the restaurant inventory/purchasing software market is $4.55B (2025) → $9.18B (2030).

Build from data that already exists and requires no staff action during service: invoices, POS sales, and recipes. Purchase-vs-theoretical-usage variance is computable without anyone weighing anything. That's the one honest path to waste insight that survives the compliance problem.

03 · How restaurants buy software

This section is the reason to be careful. It is not "there are competitors." It is that the distribution channel you'd have to sell through has already shipped the product you were going to sell through it.

2 April 2026: Square launched "Square Restaurant Inventory by MarketMan" — MarketMan's engine inside Square, single sign-on, unified billing, native POS feed, $99/month per location.

Toast has bundled xtraCHEF since 2021. As of April 2026, both dominant POS platforms ship a first-party food-costing answer one toggle away from a restaurant that already has the POS installed.

There is no headroom. Every dollar you win is taken from a vendor already installed. And 64% of restaurants name technology consolidation as a priority, with operators describing vendor fatigue from managing as many as 20 providers — you'd be selling a 21st vendor into a market actively trying to get to five.

The platform tax

Selling a $99/mo product through Toast's marketplace
Restaurant pays you $99
Toast's revenue share, 30%, in perpetuity ⚠️ −$30
Payment processing ~2.9% + 30¢ −$3
You keep $66
Restaurant's true cost $149/mo — $99 to you + $50/mo to Toast for the Restaurant Management Suite any API integration requires
  • You keep two-thirds, and the restaurant pays half again your sticker price — while selling against a bundled module they can switch on with no integration fee at all.
  • Toast's partner path is an eight-stage process requiring compliance, privacy, security and legal sign-off before development begins.
  • Square requires five live paying Square sellers before it will list you — the channel that would get you customers requires customers.
  • 26% of operators name POS integration as the #1 barrier to adopting inventory software. Without the POS sales feed there is no theoretical usage, so no variance — and variance is the entire product. A costing tool with no POS feed is a spreadsheet with a login.

So: the integration is mandatory, it's gated behind an eight-stage approval, and the gate is operated by the company selling the competing product.

The one documented solo success — and why it doesn't transfer

Recipe Cost Calculator · Daniel Wintschel

10,000+ food businesses, from $29/month, solo, no sales team, no funding, self-serve checkout, no demo call. ⚠️ self-reported. It's proof one person can build a profitable food-costing business. Almost none of it is repeatable from a standing start:

  • He owned a wholesale bakery for a decade first. Domain credibility was pre-existing, not acquired through interviews.
  • He was his own first customer for a year before selling to anyone.
  • He does not sell to restaurants. Customers are bakeries, caterers, meal-prep companies, CPG producers and food manufacturers.
  • Therefore he needs no POS integration. A bakery costing a SKU has no covers, no tickets, no Toast — the hardest technical and political dependency simply isn't in his product.
  • It took 13+ years.

The lesson isn't "a solo founder can win here." It's "the solo founder who won did it in the adjacent market that has the same math and none of the gatekeepers."

The dish suggestion is the garnish. You'd be selling the garnish against a company that owns the plate — and that now sits inside the cash register.

05 · The permit-data idea

Selling building-permit and contractor-license data as leads. You asked for more on this, so here it is — and the honest answer is that it doesn't survive.

Kills it "Permit data is an untapped lead source" A permit is a lagging indicator. By the time it's filed, the contractor is hired, the scope is set and the money is committed. You'd be selling a list of people who have already bought.
Kills it "Nobody sells this data cheaply" The low end is already saturated. PermitStack $19–49/mo · permits.llc at $0.01 per record · Apify scrapers at $0.001 per record · Shovels bundles 37 states. On the license side: the CSLB sells a $235 bulk file, Dietrich Direct sells lists from $69, and the National Contractor Index carries 1M+ records. There is no arbitrage in reselling public records that already cost a tenth of a cent.
Kills it "Contractors will pay for leads" They already do, and they hate it. Angi runs ~5.9% monthly churn — meaning it replaces most of its customer base annually — and the FTC hit HomeAdvisor with a $7.2M order over how leads were sold. You'd be entering the most litigated, highest-churn corner of small-business SaaS with a worse product.
Legal Scraping public permit portals is defensible — with one rule hiQ v. LinkedIn and Van Buren established that scraping publicly available data isn't CFAA "unauthorized access"; Meta v. Bright Data reinforced that contract claims turn on account terms. The operational rule that follows: never log in. The moment you accept terms of service, you're in contract, not just tort — and that's where scrapers actually lose.

Keep the legal rule; it generalises to any public-data product you build later. Drop the business.

06 · The questionnaire

38 questions, 20–25 minutes. Everything above is desk research and every number in it is contested. This is how you find out what's actually true in Santa Barbara. Tick questions off as you ask them — your progress is saved in this browser.

1

Ask about their life, not your idea

The moment you describe a product, everyone becomes encouraging and the data is worthless. Do not mention software, an app, or PantryChef at any point. If asked: "I'm a UCSB stats student studying how independent kitchens manage cost. I'm not selling anything." That's true, and it's the best door-opener you have.

2

Specific past events, never the future

"Would you use…?" and "Do you think…?" produce fantasy. "Walk me through the last time…" produces facts.

3

Chase money and time already spent

Did they spend money on it? Time? Have they tried to fix it before? If none of those are true, they don't have a problem — they have an opinion.

Practical: interview between 2–4pm, never during service. Ask permission to record. Buy something. Talk less than 30% of the time — silence is your best tool, let them fill it.

Where to find fifteen restaurants

Santa Barbara is unusually good for this — a dense independent scene in walking distance, and a student identity that opens doors a salesperson's wouldn't.

  • Walk in at 2:30pm on a Tuesday. Slowest hour of the slowest day. Ask for the owner or the chef.
  • Start with places you already eat at. Familiarity converts.

Decide these now, before you're emotionally invested — after fifteen interviews
  • Fewer than 5 name food cost or waste unprompted → the pivot is wrong. Follow whatever they did name.
  • Fewer than 3 have ever tried to fix it → it's a cost they've accepted, not a problem they'll pay to solve.
  • Nobody can put a number on it → you can't sell a solution to a problem nobody measures.
  • Most say delivery commissions are the real bleed → that's the actual business. Go look at it.
  • More than 5 already run their POS's inventory module → you're selling against a bundle at a price you can't match. Take the costing product to bakeries, caterers and meal-prep companies instead, where there's no POS to bundle it into.

Write the kill criteria on paper and stick them above your desk before interview one. The whole point of doing fifteen interviews is to be allowed to be wrong cheaply. Founders skip this step, hear what they hoped for in three of fifteen conversations, and build anyway.

The recommendation

08 · What to actually do

Don't Build inventory software for restaurants Five stacked reasons, any two of which would be enough: both dominant POS platforms shipped the answer · the wallet is ~$333/mo against a $48K profit · distribution costs 30% in perpetuity plus a $50/mo tax on your own customer · the mandatory POS feed is the #1 adoption barrier and is controlled by your competitor · the market wants fewer vendors, not a better 21st one.
Don't Pitch food waste reduction ~70% of the problem is plate waste you can't touch, measurement needs hardware, the buyers who pay are hotels and contract caterers rather than restaurants, nothing compels the spend, and every headline number you'd cite falls apart under scrutiny. Never cite 7:1, "$162B", or "4–10%" in a deck — any operator or investor who checks will discount everything else you said.
Do Sell costing to food businesses that aren't restaurants Bakeries, caterers, meal-prep companies, ghost kitchens, CPG producers, food trucks. Same math, same pain, no POS dependency, no marketplace gatekeeper, no bundled incumbent. This is the only path in the entire file with a documented solo success behind it.
Do Price so nobody has to talk to you $29–79/mo, self-serve, no demo call. That was Recipe Cost Calculator's entire go-to-market, and it's the only model a solo founder can actually operate while enrolled at UCSB.
Do Run the fifteen interviews before writing any code Every number above is contested, vendor-supplied, or unsourced. Fifteen conversations at $60 and one week buys you primary data nobody else has — including the delivery-mix number that doesn't exist publicly. And if the answers say "delivery commissions," you've found a bigger problem for the price of ten coffees.
Do Get a kitchen job Every durable player in this category was founded by someone who had already run the operation. Wintschel had a bakery for a decade. That's the actual prerequisite, and it's cheaper to acquire than a year of building the wrong thing.
Source quality · read this before quoting anything above

WebFetch was egress-blocked for this entire research programme. Every figure here is a search-index summary; no primary document was opened. Figures marked ⚠️ are vendor-stated or self-reported to aggregators.

A large share of food-cost benchmarks come from companies selling inventory software — Restaurant365, MarginEdge, MarketMan, Toast, meez, Supy, Apicbase, WISK — who have a direct commercial interest in making food cost sound large and mismanagement universal. Worse, a distinct cluster of 2026-dated sites recurred throughout the results (restaurantinventorytools.com, vantainsights.com, culta.ai, stockcount.io, dishcost.com and others) that reads as AI-generated SEO content for inventory-software affiliates — confident, precisely-numbered benchmarks with no attribution. Do not stake a pivot on any number appearing only in that cluster.

Genuinely independent sources used: Ohio State News and Parsa (academic), Science 2024 (UC San Diego Rady School), Nation's Restaurant News, Restaurant Business, IBISWorld, USDA ERS, EPA, ReFED, BLS, Forbes/Georgetown, and the Independent Restaurant Coalition (biased against delivery apps specifically).

The most important missing number: no survey exists quantifying what share of independents lack accurate plate costs. Only unsourced vendor assertions. The same is true of delivery-mix share for independents. Both are things your fifteen interviews would establish for the first time.
